Part A · Semantics of propositional logic

4 questions · ~12 min

L2 slides 9–16. The one idea to hold onto: an interpretation is just the set of atoms that are true, and $\models$ is computed by recursion on the formula. A4 is the highest-value item. A countermodel is the only way to show that an entailment fails.

(h)satisfiable$I=\{y\}$: $x\to y$ true, $y \to x$ false
Case split for (g)

If $x\in I$, the consequent $x$ is true, so the whole implication is true. If $x\notin I$, then $x\to y$ is true (false antecedent), so $(x\to y)\to x$ is $T\to F = F$, and an implication with a false antecedent is true. Both cases are true, so it is valid.

Why this matters: (g) is valid classically, but it has no intuitionistic proof. In natural deduction you'd need PBC, LEM or DNE (L2 slide 25). That's the same pattern as B6.

A2compute[3 marks]

L2 slide 14 evaluates $x \to (y \land z)$ under $I=\{y,z\}$.

  1. Carry the evaluation through using the recursive definition of $\models$, naming the clause used at each step.
  2. The slide asks "what other interpretations would work here?" List every interpretation over $\{x,y,z\}$ that satisfies the formula.
(a)

$I\models x\to(y\land z) \iff I\models y\land z$ or $I\nvDash x$ (clause for $\to$). Next, $I\models y\land z \iff I\models y$ and $I\models z$ (clause for $\land$). Then $I\models y\iff y\in I$, and likewise for $z$ (atom clause). Both $y$ and $z$ are in $I$, so the left disjunct holds and the formula is true. (The right disjunct holds too, since $x\notin I$.)

(b)

The formula is false exactly when $x\in I$ and $y\land z$ fails. That is three interpretations: $\{x\},\{x,y\},\{x,z\}$. Every other interpretation satisfies it:

$\emptyset,\ \{y\},\ \{z\},\ \{y,z\},\ \{x,y,z\}$

Checks$5$ models $+\ 3$ non-models $= 8 = 2^3$ ✓

A4compute[6 marks]

For each, decide whether $S\models\alpha$. If it holds, give a one-line semantic argument. If it fails, give a countermodel.

  1. $\{p\to q,\ \neg q\}\models\neg p$
  2. $\{p\to q,\ q\}\models p$
  3. $\{p\lor q,\ \neg p\}\models q$
  4. $\{p\to q\}\models q\to p$
  5. $\{p\to (q\lor r)\}\models p\to q$
  6. $\{(p\land q)\to r\}\models (p\to r)\lor(q\to r)$

For (f), suppose the conclusion is false and see what that forces each atom to be.

holds?argument / countermodel
(a)yesa model of $\neg q$ has $q$ false; if $p$ were true, $p\to q$ would be false. So $p$ is false. (Modus tollens.)
(b)no$I=\{q\}$: both premises true, $p$ false (affirming the consequent)
(c)yes$p$ is false, so $p\lor q$ forces $q$ true (disjunctive syllogism)
(d)no$I=\{q\}$: $p\to q$ true, $q\to p$ false (the converse)
(e)no$I=\{p,r\}$: premise true via $r$, $p\to q$ false
(f)yesconclusion false means $p\to r$ and $q\to r$ both false, so $p,q$ true and $r$ false. Then $(p\land q)\to r$ is false. No model of the premise falsifies the conclusion.

Why this matters: (f) surprises most people, which is exactly why you check semantically before trying to prove anything. A failed proof attempt never shows non-entailment; a countermodel does. By soundness, a countermodel also shows that no proof can exist (see B7).

Part C · First-order logic: translation and models

5 questions · ~20 min

C1compute[4 marks]

(L3 slide 9.) Over the natural numbers, with constant $0$, function $\mathit{succ}$, infix function $\times$, and predicates $\mathit{even}$, $\mathit{odd}$, $\mathit{prime}$, $=$, $\lt$, express:

  1. Every even number is followed by an odd number.
  2. For every number there is always a prime larger than it.
  3. Every number is the square of some different number.
  4. Is (c) true in $\mathbb{N}$? Justify with one witness.
(a)

$\forall X.\,(\mathit{even}(X)\to\mathit{odd}(\mathit{succ}(X)))$

(b)

$\forall X.\,\exists Y.\,(\mathit{prime}(Y)\land X\lt Y)$. The order of the quantifiers matters. $\exists Y.\,\forall X$ would claim one prime larger than every number, which is false.

(c)

$\forall X.\,\exists Y.\,(\neg(Y=X)\land X=Y\times Y)$

(d)

False. Take $X=2$: no natural number squares to $2$. ($X=0$ and $X=1$ also fail, because they are only squares of themselves, and "different" rules that out.)

How to use this: "followed by" is a function ($\mathit{succ}$), not a predicate. When the language offers a function symbol, use it. A predicate like $\mathit{next}(X,Y)$ would need extra axioms to say each number has exactly one successor.

Model answer

Why formal deduction. A knowledge-based system stores facts and rules in a formal language and derives new knowledge mechanically. Natural language is ambiguous; a logical language with fixed syntax and semantics means every conclusion can be traced back to premises and rules. That traceability is what statistical methods alone lack. It gives explanations ("why did the system conclude this?") and checkable guarantees, which matter in safety-critical and verified systems. Examples are proof assistants checking each step, the verified seL4 kernel, and Prolog answering queries from a knowledge base.

Soundness: every formula the system derives is semantically entailed: if $\Gamma\vdash\alpha$ then $\Gamma\models\alpha$. For an AI system, soundness means it never "proves" something false in every model of its knowledge base. Without it, derived conclusions can't be trusted. Natural deduction is sound. As a consequence, a countermodel shows a claimed proof must contain an error.

Completeness: every entailed formula can be derived: if $\Gamma\models\alpha$ then $\Gamma\vdash\alpha$. For a system, it means no valid conclusion is out of reach in principle. Natural deduction is complete for propositional and first-order logic. Completeness doesn't promise that a proof is easy to find, only that one exists.

Decidability: there is an algorithm that always terminates with a correct yes/no answer. Propositional validity is decidable (truth tables), though intractable in general. First-order validity is undecidable but semi-decidable: a procedure can enumerate every validity, so it eventually confirms a valid formula, but it may run forever on an invalid one. In practice, an AI reasoner over full FOL may never return. That's why practical systems restrict the language: Prolog uses definite clauses with a systematic resolution strategy, and later in the unit description logics trade expressivity for decidable reasoning.

Trade-off. The design question is choosing a logic expressive enough to represent the domain while keeping reasoning sound, complete and (ideally) decidable. These properties pull against expressivity.

Where the marks are

Three precise definitions, each stated in the form "if derivable then entailed" or its converse. FOL undecidable but semi-decidable. At least one concrete system example. The expressivity versus decidability trade-off as the closing point. A common mark loss is defining completeness as "can prove everything", which leaves out "everything valid".

G2prove[10 marks]

A student who passes a unit can enrol in any unit that has it as a prerequisite. Jane passes CITS2211. CITS2211 is a prerequisite of CITS3005.

  1. Express this in FOL using $\mathit{passes}(S,U)$, $\mathit{prereq}(V,U)$ ("$U$ is a prerequisite of $V$") and $\mathit{canEnrol}(S,V)$.
  2. Use natural deduction to show $\mathit{canEnrol}(\mathit{jane},\mathit{cits3005})$, and then $\exists U.\,\mathit{canEnrol}(\mathit{jane},U)$.
  3. Write the same knowledge as a Prolog program. What is every answer to ?- canEnrol(jane, U).?
(a)

$\forall S.\,\forall U.\,\forall V.\,(\mathit{passes}(S,U)\land\mathit{prereq}(V,U)\to\mathit{canEnrol}(S,V))$, $\ \mathit{passes}(\mathit{jane},\mathit{cits2211})$, $\ \mathit{prereq}(\mathit{cits3005},\mathit{cits2211})$

(c) 
canEnrol(S,V) :- passes(S,U), prereq(V,U).
passes(jane,cits2211).
prereq(cits3005,cits2211).

Only U = cits3005. Note the variable renaming: in the rule, V is the unit enrolled in and U is the prerequisite, while the query's U is a different variable. Prolog variables are local to each clause.

How to use this: one $\forall E$ per quantifier, in order, then $\land I$ to assemble the antecedent, then $\to E$. The universal rule is a definite clause, which is why it drops straight into Prolog with its quantifiers left implicit.

Without notes, write down:

  1. The definitions of: interpretation (propositional and FOL), environment, sentence, valid, satisfiable, entailment $S\models\alpha$, logical equivalence.
  2. The definitions of: consistent, sound, complete, decidable, semi-decidable.
  3. The side condition on each of $\forall I$, $\forall E$, $\exists I$, $\exists E$.
  4. The definitions of: literal, clause, definite clause. What each of :-, , and ; means in Prolog.
  5. Which of all this you'd put on your A4 sheet, given what the exam provides.
(a)

Propositional interpretation: $I:\mathcal{P}\to\{T,F\}$, equivalently the set of atoms that are true. FOL interpretation: a domain $D^I$, an element $c^I$ for each constant, a total function $f^I:(D^I)^{\#f}\to D^I$ for each function symbol, and $p^I:(D^I)^{\#p}\to\{\top,\bot\}$ for each predicate. Environment: $\theta:\mathit{var}\to D^I$, extended to terms. Sentence: a formula with no free variables. Valid: true in every interpretation. Satisfiable: true in some interpretation. $S\models\alpha$: every interpretation satisfying all of $S$ satisfies $\alpha$. $\alpha\equiv\beta$: $\alpha\models\beta$ and $\beta\models\alpha$.

(b)

Consistent (as on L2 slide 27): the axioms are true in every interpretation. Sound: the rules only produce conclusions true in every interpretation ($\vdash$ implies $\models$). Complete: every valid formula is provable ($\models$ implies $\vdash$). Decidable: an algorithm always halts with the correct yes/no answer. Semi-decidable: a procedure halts with "yes" on every yes-instance but may run forever on no-instances. FOL validity is semi-decidable.

(c)

$\forall I$: from $\alpha(Y)$, with $Y$ fresh (not free in any open hypothesis). $\forall E$: to $\alpha(t)$ for any term $t$ that doesn't clash with bound variables. $\exists I$: from $\alpha(t)$, same no-clash condition. $\exists E$: the box assumes $\alpha(Y)$ with $Y$ fresh, meaning not free in the conclusion $\beta$ or in any open hypothesis.

(d)

Literal: an atom or its negation. Clause: a disjunction of literals. Definite clause: exactly one positive literal, equivalent to $A_1\land\dots\land A_m\to B$. In Prolog, :- is "if" ($\leftarrow$), , is $\land$, and ; is $\lor$.

(e)

The natural deduction rules sheet is provided in the exam (L12 revision slides), so don't spend notes space copying the rules. Spend it on what the sheet won't give you: the quantifier side conditions in your own words, the "$\forall\to$, $\exists\land$" translation rule, two or three standard countermodels (C5), the soundness/completeness/decidability definitions for a G1-style question, and Prolog gotchas (closed-world failure, is/2 instantiation, overlapping clause heads).
