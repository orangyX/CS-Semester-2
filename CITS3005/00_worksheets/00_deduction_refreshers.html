Deduction drill

Sets 7–14 · CITS2211 notation · flat derivation tables, rule names written out

Every question states its full sequent in the boxed line. Premises left of ⊢, conclusion right. Work it on paper, then hit Check.

Derivations are flat — numbered lines, justification column citing line numbers and the rule in words, exactly as in the week 3 template. No subproof boxes. Conditional proof and reductio are the only rules that open an assumption, and they cite a range (3–6) because that is the only way flat notation can show the assumption closing.

Eight sets, 15–30 min each. One per morning block.

07

Short derivations

~15 min

7.1 Derive r.

p ∨ q,  ¬q,  p → r  ⊢  r
▶Check
1. p ∨ qpremise
2. ¬qpremise
3. p → rpremise
4. p1,2, disjunctive syllogism
5. r3,4, modus ponens

7.2 Derive r.

(p ∧ q) → r,  p,  q  ⊢  r
▶Check
1. (p ∧ q) → rpremise
2. ppremise
3. qpremise
4. p ∧ q2,3, conjunction
5. r1,4, modus ponens

Line 4 is the step that gets skipped. Modus ponens needs the antecedent exactly as written, not two separate facts lying nearby.

7.3 Derive s.

p → q,  q → r,  r → s,  p  ⊢  s
▶Check
1. p → qpremise
2. q → rpremise
3. r → spremise
4. ppremise
5. q1,4, modus ponens
6. r2,5, modus ponens
7. s3,6, modus ponens

Or chain with hypothetical syllogism on 1,2 then 3, then one modus ponens. Same length, neither is more correct.

7.4 Derive q. Which two rules is this, and why are they the same rule?

¬p ∨ q,  p  ⊢  q
▶Check
1. ¬p ∨ qpremise
2. ppremise
3. q1,2, disjunctive syllogism

It is also modus ponens, because ¬p ∨ q is p → q written differently. One rule, one clause, two notations — which is the point of set 10.

7.5 Derive ¬p ∧ ¬q.

¬(p ∨ q)  ⊢  ¬p ∧ ¬q
▶Check
1. ¬(p ∨ q)premise
2. ¬p ∧ ¬q1, De Morgan

Equivalences apply to any subformula in either direction. Inference rules apply to whole lines in one direction. Mixing the two up is a steady source of invalid steps.

08

Find the counterexample

~20 min
Each is invalid. Don't just say so — give the row: an assignment making every premise true and the conclusion false. That row is the proof of invalidity.

8.1

p → q,  q → p  ⊢  p
▶Check

p = F, q = F. Both conditionals hold vacuously; the conclusion is false.

Knowing p and q stand or fall together says nothing about which way they went.

8.2

p ∨ q,  p  ⊢  ¬q
▶Check

p = T, q = T. Both premises hold; ¬q is false.

Trap — ∨ is inclusive. "p or q" permits both. English "or" often implies exactly one, and that leak is where this fallacy comes from. For exclusive you must write (p ∨ q) ∧ ¬(p ∧ q).

8.3

p → (q ∨ r),  ¬q  ⊢  ¬p
▶Check

p = T, q = F, r = T. Premise 1 is T → (F ∨ T) = T; premise 2 holds; ¬p is false.

Modus tollens needs the whole consequent negated. Killing q leaves r holding q ∨ r up alone — you would need ¬q ∧ ¬r.

8.4

p → q  ⊢  ¬p → ¬q
▶Check

p = F, q = T. Premise is F → T = T; conclusion is T → F = F.

This is the inverse. Only the contrapositive ¬q → ¬p is safe.

8.5 Give an interpretation, not a truth-table row.

∀x.(P(x) → Q(x)),  Q(a)  ⊢  P(a)
▶Check

Domain {a}, with P(a) false and Q(a) true. Premise 1 is F → T = T; premise 2 holds; the conclusion fails.

Affirming the consequent with a quantifier as a disguise. The counterexample here is an interpretation — a domain plus an assignment to each predicate — rather than a row. Keep the domain as small as you can; one element is usually enough.

09

Conditional proof and reductio

~25 min

Conditional proof. To prove A → B: assume A, derive B, discharge — the assumption goes, you keep A → B.

Reductio. To prove ¬A: assume A, derive a contradiction, discharge, keep ¬A.

Mark the line assumption and cite the closing range. An assumption still open at the end is an error, not a stylistic quirk.

9.1 Prove using conditional proof rather than hypothetical syllogism.

p → q,  q → r  ⊢  p → r
▶Check
1. p → qpremise
2. q → rpremise
3. passumption
4. q1,3, modus ponens
5. r2,4, modus ponens
6. p → r3–5, conditional proof

This is why hypothetical syllogism is valid. Every derived rule is a packaged proof like this one.

9.2 Prove.

p → q,  p → ¬q  ⊢  ¬p
▶Check
1. p → qpremise
2. p → ¬qpremise
3. passumption
4. q1,3, modus ponens
5. ¬q2,3, modus ponens
6. q ∧ ¬q4,5, conjunction (⊥)
7. ¬p3–6, reductio

If one assumption entails both a thing and its negation, the assumption is dead. This is the engine behind proof by contradiction.

9.3 Prove. Name the equivalence this establishes.

p → (q → r)  ⊢  (p ∧ q) → r
▶Check
1. p → (q → r)premise
2. p ∧ qassumption
3. p2, simplification
4. q2, simplification
5. q → r1,3, modus ponens
6. r5,4, modus ponens
7. (p ∧ q) → r2–6, conditional proof

This is exportation, and it runs both ways: (p ∧ q) → r ≡ p → (q → r).

9.4 Prove.

¬p  ⊢  ¬(p ∧ q)
▶Check
1. ¬ppremise
2. p ∧ qassumption
3. p2, simplification
4. p ∧ ¬p3,1, conjunction (⊥)
5. ¬(p ∧ q)2–4, reductio

9.5 Prove from no premises.

⊢  p → (q → p)
▶Check
1. passumption
2. qassumption
3. p1, reiteration
4. q → p2–3, conditional proof
5. p → (q → p)1–4, conditional proof

Nested assumptions, discharged innermost first. A theorem provable from zero premises is a tautology.

10

Resolution

~25 min

The rule. From A ∨ x and B ∨ ¬x, infer A ∨ B. Exactly one complementary pair cancels per step; everything else survives. Same sign on both sides means no inference.

Refutation. To show premises ⊨ conclusion: negate the conclusion, add it in, convert to clauses, resolve until you reach the empty clause □.

10.1 Convert each to clause form.

p → q     ¬(p ∧ q)     ¬(p ∨ q)     p ↔ q
▶Check
p → q{¬p, q}
¬(p ∧ q){¬p, ¬q}
¬(p ∨ q){¬p} and {¬q} — two separate clauses
p ↔ q{¬p, q} and {¬q, p}

A clause is a disjunction of literals. A top-level conjunction splits into separate clauses — that is what CNF is for.

10.2 Convert to CNF and list the clauses.

¬(p → q) ∨ (r → s)
▶Check
¬(¬p ∨ q) ∨ (¬r ∨ s)arrows out
(p ∧ ¬q) ∨ (¬r ∨ s)De Morgan
(p ∨ ¬r ∨ s) ∧ (¬q ∨ ¬r ∨ s)distribute

Clauses {p, ¬r, s} and {¬q, ¬r, s}.

Fixed order every time: arrows out → negations inward → distribute ∨ over ∧. A rewriting algorithm, not a puzzle — which is exactly why it is automatable.

10.3 Show by resolution refutation.

p → q,  q → r  ⊨  p → r
▶Check

Negated conclusion ¬(p → r) = p ∧ ¬r.

1. {¬p, q}premise
2. {¬q, r}premise
3. {p}negated conclusion
4. {¬r}negated conclusion
5. {q}1,3, resolution on p
6. {r}2,5, resolution on q
7. □4,6, resolution on r

10.4 Show by resolution refutation.

p ∨ q,  ¬p ∨ r,  ¬q ∨ r  ⊨  r
▶Check
1. {p, q}premise
2. {¬p, r}premise
3. {¬q, r}premise
4. {¬r}negated conclusion
5. {q, r}1,2, resolution on p
6. {r}5,3, resolution on q, factored
7. □6,4, resolution on r

Line 6 shows factoring — a clause is a set, so the repeated r collapses. A prover that does not factor can loop forever producing longer duplicates.

10.5 Which pairs can be resolved, and on which literal?

{¬p, q} with {¬p, r}      {p, q} with {¬p, ¬q}
▶Check

First pair: no. ¬p appears in both with the same sign, and q, r are unrelated. No complementary pair, no resolvent.

Second pair: yes, two ways — but on one literal only. On p you get {q, ¬q}; on q you get {p, ¬p}. Both tautologous, both useless — correctly so, since the two clauses together are satisfiable.

Trap — cancelling two pairs at once. Resolving {p, q} and {¬p, ¬q} on both literals hands you □ and claims a contradiction. There isn't one — p = T, q = F satisfies both clauses. Exactly one complementary pair per step.
11

Translate, then derive

~20 min
No sequent given — producing it is the first half of the exercise. Choose your letters, write the sequent, then derive it or break it.

11.1 If the build passes we deploy. We did not deploy. Either the build passed or the linter failed. Therefore the linter failed.

▶Check

b = build passes, d = we deploy, l = linter failed.

b → d,  ¬d,  b ∨ l  ⊢  l
1. b → dpremise
2. ¬dpremise
3. b ∨ lpremise
4. ¬b1,2, modus tollens
5. l3,4, disjunctive syllogism

Valid.

11.2 Either the mutex is held or the queue is empty. The queue is not empty. If the mutex is held, the worker is waiting. Therefore the worker is waiting.

▶Check
m ∨ e,  ¬e,  m → w  ⊢  w
1. m ∨ epremise
2. ¬epremise
3. m → wpremise
4. m1,2, disjunctive syllogism
5. w3,4, modus ponens

Valid — and structurally identical to 7.1. Once translated the subject matter vanishes entirely. That is the point of formalising: two problems that read nothing alike turn out to be the same two lines.

11.3 Every process holding a lock is blocked. Process p is not blocked. Therefore p is not holding a lock.

▶Check
∀x.(H(x) → B(x)),  ¬B(p)  ⊢  ¬H(p)
1. ∀x.(H(x) → B(x))premise
2. ¬B(p)premise
3. H(p) → B(p)1, forall elimination
4. ¬H(p)3,2, modus tollens

Valid.

11.4 If the graph has a cycle, depth-first search finds a back edge. Depth-first search found a back edge. Therefore the graph has a cycle.

▶Check
c → b,  b  ⊢  c

Invalid — affirming the consequent. Counterexample c = F, b = T.

The awkward part: for directed graphs the conclusion happens to be true, because back edge and cycle really are equivalent. This argument does not establish it — the missing premise is the converse, and without it you arrived by luck. A true conclusion reached by an invalid argument.

12

Find the broken step

~20 min
Each derivation has at least one invalid line. Name the line, name the rule misapplied, and say whether the conclusion is salvageable.

12.1

p → q,  q → r,  r  ⊢  p
1. p → qpremise
2. q → rpremise
3. rpremise
4. q2,3, modus ponens
5. p1,4, modus ponens
▶Check

Lines 4 and 5 are both invalid. Modus ponens runs antecedent to consequent: from q → r and q you get r. Having r says nothing about q. Line 5 repeats the error.

Not salvageable — p = F, q = F, r = T satisfies all three premises with p false.

12.2

p ∨ q,  q  ⊢  ¬p
1. p ∨ qpremise
2. qpremise
3. ¬p1,2, disjunctive syllogism
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Disjunctive syllogism needs a negated disjunct: from p ∨ q and ¬q you get p. An affirmed disjunct yields nothing, because ∨ is inclusive.

Counterexample p = T, q = T. This is 8.2 in a different costume.

12.3

(∀x.P(x)) → Q,  P(a)  ⊢  Q
1. (∀x.P(x)) → Qpremise
2. P(a)premise
3. Q1,2, modus ponens
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. The antecedent of premise 1 is ∀x.P(x) — every object satisfies P. Premise 2 supplies one instance, and one instance is not the universal.

Counterexample: domain {a, b} with P(a) true, P(b) false, Q false. Premise 1 holds vacuously, premise 2 holds, Q is false.

12.4

p → q,  ¬p ∨ r  ⊢  q ∨ r
1. p → qpremise
2. ¬p ∨ rpremise
3. q ∨ r1,2, resolution
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Premise 1 as a clause is {¬p, q}; premise 2 is {¬p, r}. The literal ¬p appears in both with the same sign, so no resolution step exists.

Counterexample p = F, q = F, r = F: premise 1 is F → F = T, premise 2 is T ∨ F = T, conclusion is F.

12.5

⊢  p ∨ q
1. passumption
2. p ∨ q1, addition
3. p ∨ q2, discharge
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Discharging does not delete an assumption, it records it in the conclusion. Lines 1–2 establish p → (p ∨ q) by conditional proof.

As written, line 3 asserts p ∨ q from nothing, which is false when both are false. An assumption that quietly evaporates is the most common error in natural-deduction proofs — and it has an exact analogue in 13.6.

13

Quantifier introduction and elimination

~30 min

Exists elimination is flat here. Unpack ∃x.φ(x) to φ(a) for a fresh a, cite "exists elimination", carry on. No box, no discharge line — the freshness condition still applies, it is just enforced by convention rather than displayed.

"Arbitrary" / "fresh" means: the name appears in no premise and no open assumption. It is a name you invented for this proof and about which you assumed nothing.

13.1 Derive.

∀x.(P(x) → Q(x)),  P(a)  ⊢  Q(a)
▶Check
1. ∀x.(P(x) → Q(x))premise
2. P(a)premise
3. P(a) → Q(a)1, forall elimination
4. Q(a)3,2, modus ponens

Forall elimination is unconditional — you may instantiate at any term, including one already named in the premises. That is why line 3 is fine even though a appears in premise 2.

13.2 Derive. What background assumption does this rely on?

∀x.P(x)  ⊢  ∃x.P(x)
▶Check
1. ∀x.P(x)premise
2. P(a)1, forall elimination
3. ∃x.P(x)2, exists introduction

It relies on the domain being non-empty. Standard first-order logic builds that in as a convention, so the inference is valid — but over an empty domain ∀x.P(x) is vacuously true while ∃x.P(x) is false.

Worth knowing because some knowledge-representation formalisms and some database semantics do not assume it.

13.3 Derive. Justify the forall introduction step explicitly.

∀x.(P(x) → Q(x)),  ∀x.P(x)  ⊢  ∀x.Q(x)
▶Check
1. ∀x.(P(x) → Q(x))premise
2. ∀x.P(x)premise
3. P(a) → Q(a)1, forall elimination (a arbitrary)
4. P(a)2, forall elimination
5. Q(a)3,4, modus ponens
6. ∀x.Q(x)5, forall introduction (a arbitrary)

Line 6 is legal because a occurs in no premise and no open assumption — it was introduced at line 3 purely to have something to work with, and nothing specific was ever assumed about it.

This is the formal version of "let n be an arbitrary integer … therefore it holds for all integers".

13.4 Derive.

∃x.P(x),  ∀x.(P(x) → Q(x))  ⊢  ∃x.Q(x)
▶Check
1. ∃x.P(x)premise
2. ∀x.(P(x) → Q(x))premise
3. P(a)1, exists elimination (a fresh)
4. P(a) → Q(a)2, forall elimination
5. Q(a)4,3, modus ponens
6. ∃x.Q(x)5, exists introduction

The shape of every exists elimination: something satisfies P, so give it a fresh name, reason with it, and make sure the final conclusion no longer mentions the name.

Line 6 matters. You could not stop at Q(a), because a was never a real object — only a placeholder for whichever witness premise 1 promised. This is exactly the shape of the week 3 question 4 template.

13.5 Find the broken step and give an interpretation that refutes it.

P(a)  ⊢  ∀x.P(x)
1. P(a)premise
2. ∀x.P(x)1, forall introduction
▶Check

Line 2 violates the arbitrariness condition. The name a occurs in premise 1, so it is not arbitrary — it is a specific object you were handed information about.

Interpretation: domain {a, b} with P(a) true and P(b) false. Premise holds, conclusion fails.

The everyday version: "this array is sorted, therefore every array is sorted".

13.6 Find the broken step and give an interpretation that refutes it.

∃x.P(x),  ∃x.¬P(x)  ⊢  ⊥
1. ∃x.P(x)premise
2. ∃x.¬P(x)premise
3. P(a)1, exists elimination
4. ¬P(a)2, exists elimination
5. ⊥3,4
▶Check

Line 4 violates the freshness condition. Line 3 already used a, so premise 2's witness must get a different name — say b.

Interpretation: domain {a, b} with P(a) true and P(b) false. Both premises hold and there is no contradiction whatsoever. Done correctly you obtain P(a) and ¬P(b) — two different objects, perfectly consistent.

Trap — reusing a witness name. Two separate existential statements almost never promise the same witness. Every exists elimination gets its own new letter. This is the most common quantifier error after forall introduction on a non-arbitrary name, and it is the direct analogue of 12.5: an assumption used as though it were a fact.

13.7 Derive. All four quantifier rules appear.

∃x.∀y.F(x,y)  ⊢  ∀y.∃x.F(x,y)
▶Check
1. ∃x.∀y.F(x,y)premise
2. ∀y.F(a,y)1, exists elimination (a fresh)
3. F(a,b)2, forall elimination (b arbitrary)
4. ∃x.F(x,b)3, exists introduction
5. ∀y.∃x.F(x,y)4, forall introduction (b arbitrary)

Line 5 is legal because b is arbitrary. Note a is not arbitrary at that point — it is the assumed witness — which is exactly why the conclusion generalises over y and not over x.

13.8 Show the converse is invalid, and say exactly which rule application fails.

∀y.∃x.F(x,y)  ⊢  ∃x.∀y.F(x,y)
▶Check

Interpretation. Domain ℕ, with F(x,y) reading x > y. Every y has some larger x, so the premise holds. No single x exceeds every natural number, so the conclusion fails.

Where a proof attempt breaks: instantiate the premise at an arbitrary b to get ∃x.F(x,b), then exists elimination gives a witness a with F(a,b). Reaching the goal now needs forall introduction on b — but b is no longer arbitrary, because a was chosen after seeing b and the open assumption F(a,b) mentions it.

That failed side condition is the ∀∃ versus ∃∀ asymmetry, stated as a rule violation rather than as intuition: the witness was allowed to depend on b, so it cannot be lifted out in front.

14

Name the rule

~15 min
Premises above the bar, conclusion below — the notation your week 3 sheet uses in question 5. Name the rule being applied.

14.1

p ∧ q(p ∧ q) → (r ∨ s)
r ∨ s
▶Check

Modus ponens, with p ∧ q as a single compound antecedent. The conjunction is not being decomposed at all — it is just the shape of the antecedent.

14.2

¬(¬(p ∧ (q ∨ r)))
p ∧ (q ∨ r)
▶Check

Double negation elimination. An equivalence, not an inference rule — which is why it can be applied to a subformula and run in either direction.

14.3

∀x.(P(x) → Q(x))P(a)
Q(a)
▶Check

Two steps compressed under one bar: forall elimination to get P(a) → Q(a), then modus ponens.

Worth spotting — a single bar does not have to mean a single rule.

14.4

p ∧ q ∧ r¬(p ∧ q ∧ r)
s
▶Check

From a contradiction, anything follows — ex falso quodlibet.

The giveaway is that s shares no letters with the premises. Any rule that produces a conclusion unrelated to its inputs is either this or a mistake.

14.5

F(a,b)
∃x.F(x,b)
▶Check

Exists introduction. Note b stays free — only a was generalised. You may generalise on one argument position and leave the others alone.

Sets 7 and 11 are ordinary derivation practice. Sets 8 and 12 move the needle most — producing a counterexample and diagnosing a broken step are harder than writing a correct proof, and closer to what mid-sem questions ask. Set 10 is the machinery CITS3005 is built on. Set 13 is the one to do slowly: the two side conditions are the entire difficulty, and 13.8 is where the ∃∀ versus ∀∃ distinction becomes mechanical rather than a matter of squinting at it.
