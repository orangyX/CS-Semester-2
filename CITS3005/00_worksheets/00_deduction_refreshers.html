Deduction drill

Sets 7–13 · derivations, counterexamples, resolution, quantifier rules · companion to the logic refresher

Every question states its full sequent in the boxed line: premises on the left of ⊢, conclusion on the right. Read that first — it's the whole problem. The numbered lines below it are just the same premises laid out ready to build on.

One procedure underneath all of it. Valid iff there is no row where every premise is true and the conclusion is false. Assume the conclusion false, force the premises true, hunt for a contradiction. Contradiction → valid. Row completes → that row is your counterexample.

Convert every arrow to a disjunction before you start. p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q. Most rules below collapse into one move once the arrows are gone.

07

Short derivations

~15 min

Rules in play. MP modus ponens · MT modus tollens · DS disjunctive syllogism · HS hypothetical syllogism · Simp simplification (p ∧ q ⊢ p) · Conj conjunction (p, q ⊢ p ∧ q) · Add addition (p ⊢ p ∨ q).

Cite a rule and line numbers on every step. "Obvious" earns no marks and hides errors.

7.1 Derive r.

p ∨ q, ¬q, p → r ⊢ r 1. p ∨ q premise 2. ¬q premise 3. p → r premise
▶Check
4. p 1, 2 DS 5. r 3, 4 MP

7.2 Derive r.

(p ∧ q) → r, p, q ⊢ r 1. (p ∧ q) → r premise 2. p premise 3. q premise
▶Check
4. p ∧ q 2, 3 Conj 5. r 1, 4 MP

Line 4 is the step people skip. MP needs the antecedent exactly as written — p ∧ q, not two separate facts lying around nearby.

7.3 Derive s.

p → q, q → r, r → s, p ⊢ s 1. p → q premise 2. q → r premise 3. r → s premise 4. p premise
▶Check
5. q 1, 4 MP 6. r 2, 5 MP 7. s 3, 6 MP

Or chain first: HS on 1,2 gives p → r; HS with 3 gives p → s; then one MP. Same length, neither is more correct.

7.4 Derive q. What is this rule "really"?

¬p ∨ q, p ⊢ q 1. ¬p ∨ q premise 2. p premise
▶Check
3. q 1, 2 DS

It's also modus ponens, because ¬p ∨ q is just p → q wearing a different hat. MP and DS are one rule applied to one clause written two ways — which is the whole point of set 10.

7.5 Derive ¬p ∧ ¬q.

¬(p ∨ q) ⊢ ¬p ∧ ¬q 1. ¬(p ∨ q) premise
▶Check
2. ¬p ∧ ¬q 1 De Morgan

Equivalences (De Morgan, double negation, p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q) apply to any subformula, in either direction. Inference rules like MP apply to whole lines, in one direction. Confusing the two is a steady source of invalid steps.

08

Break it — find the counterexample

~20 min
Each of these is invalid. Don't just say so — produce the row: an assignment making every premise true and the conclusion false. That row is the proof of invalidity.

8.1 Show invalid.

p → q, q → p ⊢ p
▶Check

p = F, q = F. Both conditionals hold vacuously; the conclusion is false.

Knowing p and q stand or fall together tells you nothing about which way they went.

8.2 Show invalid.

p ∨ q, p ⊢ ¬q
▶Check

p = T, q = T. Premise 1 holds, premise 2 holds, conclusion ¬q is false.

Trap — ∨ is inclusive. "p or q" permits both. English "or" often implies exactly one, and that leak is where this fallacy comes from. For exclusive you must write (p ∨ q) ∧ ¬(p ∧ q).

8.3 Show invalid.

p → (q ∨ r), ¬q ⊢ ¬p
▶Check

p = T, q = F, r = T. Premise 1 is T → (F ∨ T) = T ✓, premise 2 ✓, conclusion ¬p = F.

Modus tollens needs the whole consequent negated. Killing q leaves r holding q ∨ r up on its own. You'd need ¬q ∧ ¬r.

8.4 Show invalid.

p → q ⊢ ¬p → ¬q
▶Check

p = F, q = T. Premise is F → T = T ✓, conclusion is T → F = F.

This is the inverse — question 3.1 in the first worksheet. Only the contrapositive ¬q → ¬p is safe.

8.5 Show invalid.

∀x (P(x) → Q(x)), Q(a) ⊢ P(a)
▶Check

Domain {a}, with P(a) false and Q(a) true. Premise 1 becomes F → T = T ✓, premise 2 ✓, conclusion false.

Affirming the consequent wearing a quantifier as a disguise. The counterexample for a quantified argument is a model — a domain plus an interpretation of each predicate — rather than a truth-table row. Keep the domain as small as you can; one element is usually enough.

09

Assumptions — conditional proof and reductio

~25 min

Conditional proof (CP). To prove A → B: assume A, derive B, then discharge — the assumption goes away and you keep A → B.

Reductio (RAA). To prove ¬A: assume A, derive a contradiction, discharge, keep ¬A.

Indent assumed lines and mark where the assumption closes. An assumption still open at the end is an error, not a stylistic quirk.

9.1 Prove using CP rather than HS.

p → q, q → r ⊢ p → r
▶Check
1. p → q premise 2. q → r premise 3. │ p assume 4. │ q 1, 3 MP 5. │ r 2, 4 MP 6. p → r 3–5 CP

This is why hypothetical syllogism is valid. Every derived rule is a packaged-up proof like this one.

9.2 Prove.

p → q, p → ¬q ⊢ ¬p
▶Check
1. p → q premise 2. p → ¬q premise 3. │ p assume 4. │ q 1, 3 MP 5. │ ¬q 2, 3 MP 6. │ q ∧ ¬q 4, 5 Conj ⊥ 7. ¬p 3–6 RAA

If one assumption entails both a thing and its negation, the assumption is dead. This is the engine behind proof by contradiction — question 6.3 in the first worksheet is this exact shape, with "√2 is rational" as line 3.

9.3 Prove.

p → (q → r) ⊢ (p ∧ q) → r
▶Check
1. p → (q → r) premise 2. │ p ∧ q assume 3. │ p 2 Simp 4. │ q 2 Simp 5. │ q → r 1, 3 MP 6. │ r 5, 4 MP 7. (p ∧ q) → r 2–6 CP

This is exportation, and it runs both ways: (p ∧ q) → r ≡ p → (q → r). Compare 2.4 in the first worksheet — that's the equivalence stated, this is it proved.

9.4 Prove.

¬p ⊢ ¬(p ∧ q)
▶Check
1. ¬p premise 2. │ p ∧ q assume 3. │ p 2 Simp 4. │ p ∧ ¬p 3, 1 Conj ⊥ 5. ¬(p ∧ q) 2–4 RAA

9.5 Prove from no premises at all.

⊢ p → (q → p)
▶Check
1. │ p assume 2. │ │ q assume 3. │ │ p 1 reiteration 4. │ q → p 2–3 CP 5. p → (q → p) 1–4 CP

Nested assumptions, discharged innermost-first. A theorem provable from zero premises is a tautology — a truth table would confirm it too, but the derivation shows why rather than just that.

10

Resolution

~25 min

The rule. From A ∨ x and B ∨ ¬x, infer A ∨ B. One complementary pair cancels; everything else survives. Same sign on both sides means no inference.

Refutation procedure. To show premises ⊨ conclusion: negate the conclusion, add it to the premises, convert everything to clauses, resolve until you reach the empty clause □. Reaching □ means the set is unsatisfiable, which means the original argument was valid.

10.1 Convert each to clause form.

p → q ¬(p ∧ q) ¬(p ∨ q) p ↔ q
▶Check
p → q ⇒ {¬p, q} ¬(p ∧ q) ⇒ {¬p, ¬q} ¬(p ∨ q) ⇒ {¬p} and {¬q} two separate clauses p ↔ q ⇒ {¬p, q} and {¬q, p}

A clause is a disjunction of literals. A conjunction at the top level splits into separate clauses — that's what CNF is for.

10.2 Convert to CNF and list the clauses.

¬(p → q) ∨ (r → s)
▶Check
¬(p → q) ∨ (r → s) = ¬(¬p ∨ q) ∨ (¬r ∨ s) arrows out = (p ∧ ¬q) ∨ (¬r ∨ s) De Morgan = (p ∨ ¬r ∨ s) ∧ (¬q ∨ ¬r ∨ s) distribute

Two clauses: {p, ¬r, s} and {¬q, ¬r, s}.

Fixed order every time: arrows out → negations inward → distribute ∨ over ∧. A rewriting algorithm, not a puzzle — which is exactly why it's automatable.

10.3 Show by resolution refutation.

p → q, q → r ⊨ p → r
▶Check

Negated conclusion: ¬(p → r) = p ∧ ¬r, giving clauses {p} and {¬r}.

1. {¬p, q} premise 2. {¬q, r} premise 3. {p} ¬conclusion 4. {¬r} ¬conclusion 5. {q} 1, 3 resolve on p 6. {r} 2, 5 resolve on q 7. □ 4, 6 resolve on r

Empty clause reached, so the argument is valid.

10.4 Show by resolution refutation.

p ∨ q, ¬p ∨ r, ¬q ∨ r ⊨ r
▶Check
1. {p, q} premise 2. {¬p, r} premise 3. {¬q, r} premise 4. {¬r} ¬conclusion 5. {q, r} 1, 2 resolve on p 6. {r, r} = {r} 5, 3 resolve on q 7. □ 6, 4 resolve on r

Line 6 shows factoring — a clause is a set, so a repeated literal collapses. Worth knowing: a prover that doesn't factor can loop forever producing longer and longer duplicates.

This is proof by cases (5.3 in the first worksheet) done mechanically.

10.5 Which of these pairs can be resolved, and on what?

{¬p, q} with {¬p, r} {p, q} with {¬p, ¬q}
▶Check

First pair: no. ¬p appears in both with the same sign, and q, r are unrelated. No complementary pair, no resolvent.

Second pair: yes, two ways — but on one literal only. On p you get {q, ¬q}; on q you get {p, ¬p}. Both are tautologous and both are useless, which is correct: the two clauses together are perfectly satisfiable.

Trap — cancelling two pairs at once. Resolving {p, q} and {¬p, ¬q} on both literals hands you □ and claims a contradiction. There isn't one — take p = T, q = F and both clauses hold. Exactly one complementary pair per step.
11

Translate, then derive

~20 min
No sequent given — producing it is the first half of the exercise. Choose your letters, write the sequent, then derive or break it.

11.1 "If the build passes we deploy. We didn't deploy. Either the build passed or the linter failed. So the linter failed."

▶Check

b = build passes, d = we deploy, l = linter failed.

b → d, ¬d, b ∨ l ⊢ l 1. b → d premise 2. ¬d premise 3. b ∨ l premise 4. ¬b 1, 2 MT 5. l 3, 4 DS

Valid.

11.2 "Either the mutex is held or the queue is empty. The queue isn't empty. If the mutex is held, the worker is waiting. So the worker is waiting."

▶Check
m ∨ e, ¬e, m → w ⊢ w 1. m ∨ e premise 2. ¬e premise 3. m → w premise 4. m 1, 2 DS 5. w 3, 4 MP

Valid — and structurally identical to 7.1. Once translated, the subject matter vanishes entirely. That's the point of formalising: two problems that read nothing alike turn out to be the same two lines.

11.3 "Every process holding a lock is blocked. Process P is not blocked. So P is not holding a lock."

▶Check
∀x (H(x) → B(x)), ¬B(p) ⊢ ¬H(p) 1. ∀x (H(x) → B(x)) premise 2. ¬B(p) premise 3. H(p) → B(p) 1 ∀E 4. ¬H(p) 3, 2 MT

Valid. Line 3 is the new move — universal elimination, covered properly in set 13.

11.4 "If the graph has a cycle, DFS finds a back edge. DFS found a back edge. Therefore the graph has a cycle."

▶Check
c → b, b ⊢ c

Invalid — affirming the consequent. Counterexample: c = F, b = T satisfies both premises and falsifies the conclusion.

The awkward part: for directed graphs the conclusion is true, because back edge and cycle really are equivalent. But this argument doesn't establish it — the missing premise is the converse, and without it you arrived by luck.

The validity/soundness split from 5.5, running the other direction: a true conclusion reached by an invalid argument.

12

Find the broken step

~20 min
Each proof below has at least one invalid line. Name the line, say which rule was misapplied, and say whether the conclusion is salvageable.

12.1

p → q, q → r, r ⊢ p 1. p → q premise 2. q → r premise 3. r premise 4. q 2, 3 MP 5. p 1, 4 MP
▶Check

Lines 4 and 5 are both invalid. MP runs antecedent-to-consequent: from q → r and q you get r. Having r tells you nothing about q. Line 5 repeats the mistake.

Not salvageable — p = F, q = F, r = T satisfies all three premises with p false.

12.2

p ∨ q, q ⊢ ¬p 1. p ∨ q premise 2. q premise 3. ¬p 1, 2 DS
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Disjunctive syllogism needs a negated disjunct: from p ∨ q and ¬q you get p. An affirmed disjunct yields nothing, because ∨ is inclusive.

Counterexample: p = T, q = T. This is 8.2 in a different costume.

12.3

(∀x P(x)) → Q, P(a) ⊢ Q 1. (∀x P(x)) → Q premise 2. P(a) premise 3. Q 1, 2 MP
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. The antecedent of premise 1 is ∀x P(x) — every object satisfies P. Premise 2 gives one instance, and one instance is not the universal.

Counterexample: domain {a, b} with P(a) true, P(b) false, Q false. Premise 1 holds vacuously, premise 2 holds, Q is false.

Instantiation runs ∀ → instance only. Going back up needs the instance to be arbitrary — see 13.5.

12.4

p → q, ¬p ∨ r ⊢ q ∨ r 1. p → q premise 2. ¬p ∨ r premise 3. q ∨ r 1, 2 resolution
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Rewrite premise 1 as the clause {¬p, q}. Premise 2 is {¬p, r}. ¬p appears in both with the same sign — no complementary pair, so no resolution step exists.

Counterexample: p = F, q = F, r = F. Premise 1 is F → F = T ✓, premise 2 is T ∨ F = T ✓, conclusion F ∨ F = F.

Check signs before every resolution step. It's the one place the rule can be misapplied, and it's why 10.5 exists.

12.5

⊢ p ∨ q 1. │ p assume 2. │ p ∨ q 1 Add 3. p ∨ q 2 discharge
▶Check

Line 3 is invalid. Discharging an assumption doesn't just delete it — it has to be recorded in the conclusion. What lines 1–2 actually establish is p → (p ∨ q), by conditional proof.

As written, line 3 asserts p ∨ q outright from nothing, which is false whenever both are false.

Every assumption you open must be closed by CP or RAA, and closing it always changes the formula. An assumption that quietly evaporates is the most common error in natural-deduction proofs — and it has an exact analogue in 13.6.

13

Quantifier rules — introduction and elimination

~30 min

Four rules, and the only thing you have to remember is which two carry a side condition.

RuleFromInferCondition
∀E elim.∀x φ(x)φ(t)none — any term t
∃I intro.φ(t)∃x φ(x)none — any term t
∀I intro.φ(a)∀x φ(x)a arbitrary
∃E elim.∃x φ(x) + proof of ψ from φ(a)ψa fresh, not in ψ

"Arbitrary" / "fresh" means: the name appears in no premise and no open assumption. It is a name you invented for this proof and about which you assumed nothing.

The pattern to hold onto: going down is free, going up is restricted. Dropping a ∀ to an instance and lifting an instance to a ∃ both weaken the claim, so they need no permission. Lifting an instance to a ∀, or reasoning from a ∃ whose witness you can't name, both need the eigenvariable condition.

13.1 Derive. (∀E then MP.)

∀x (P(x) → Q(x)), P(a) ⊢ Q(a)
▶Check
1. ∀x (P(x) → Q(x)) premise 2. P(a) premise 3. P(a) → Q(a) 1 ∀E 4. Q(a) 3, 2 MP

∀E is unconditional — you may instantiate at any term, including one already mentioned in the premises. That's why line 3 is fine even though a appears in premise 2.

13.2 Derive. What background assumption does this rely on?

∀x P(x) ⊢ ∃x P(x)
▶Check
1. ∀x P(x) premise 2. P(a) 1 ∀E 3. ∃x P(x) 2 ∃I

It relies on the domain being non-empty. Standard first-order logic builds that in as a convention, so the inference is valid — but over an empty domain ∀x P(x) is vacuously true while ∃x P(x) is false.

Worth knowing because some KR formalisms and some database semantics don't assume it.

13.3 Derive. Justify the ∀I step explicitly.

∀x (P(x) → Q(x)), ∀x P(x) ⊢ ∀x Q(x)
▶Check
1. ∀x (P(x) → Q(x)) premise 2. ∀x P(x) premise 3. P(a) → Q(a) 1 ∀E a arbitrary 4. P(a) 2 ∀E 5. Q(a) 3, 4 MP 6. ∀x Q(x) 5 ∀I a arbitrary ✓

The ∀I at line 6 is legal because a appears in no premise and no open assumption — it was a name introduced at line 3 purely to have something to work with, and nothing specific was ever assumed about it.

This is the formal version of the everyday "let n be an arbitrary integer… therefore it holds for all integers." Set 6 of the first worksheet used that move constantly without naming it.

13.4 Derive. This one needs ∃E.

∃x P(x), ∀x (P(x) → Q(x)) ⊢ ∃x Q(x)
▶Check
1. ∃x P(x) premise 2. ∀x (P(x) → Q(x)) premise 3. │ P(a) assume, a fresh 4. │ P(a) → Q(a) 2 ∀E 5. │ Q(a) 4, 3 MP 6. │ ∃x Q(x) 5 ∃I 7. ∃x Q(x) 1, 3–6 ∃E

The shape of every ∃E: something satisfies P — give it a fresh name, reason with it, and make sure your final conclusion no longer mentions the name.

Line 6 matters. Without it the subproof would end at Q(a), and you can't discharge to Q(a) because a was never a real object — just a placeholder for whichever witness premise 1 promised.

13.5 Find the broken step and give a counter-model.

P(a) ⊢ ∀x P(x) 1. P(a) premise 2. ∀x P(x) 1 ∀I
▶Check

Line 2 violates the arbitrariness condition. a occurs in premise 1, so it is not an arbitrary name — it's a specific object you were handed information about.

Counter-model: domain {a, b} with P(a) true and P(b) false. Premise holds, conclusion fails.

The everyday version: "this array is sorted, therefore every array is sorted."

13.6 Find the broken step and give a counter-model.

∃x P(x), ∃x ¬P(x) ⊢ ⊥ 1. ∃x P(x) premise 2. ∃x ¬P(x) premise 3. │ P(a) assume from 1, a fresh 4. │ ¬P(a) assume from 2 5. │ ⊥ 3, 4
▶Check

Line 4 violates the freshness condition. Line 3 already used a, so a is no longer fresh when premise 2 is unpacked. Premise 2's witness must get a different name — say b.

Counter-model: domain {a, b} with P(a) true, P(b) false. Both premises hold and there is no contradiction whatsoever.

Done correctly you get P(a) and ¬P(b) — two different objects, perfectly consistent.

Trap — reusing a witness name. Two separate ∃ statements almost never promise the same witness. Every ∃E gets its own new letter. This is the most common quantifier error after ∀I on a non-arbitrary name, and it's the direct analogue of 12.5: an assumption used as though it were a fact.

13.7 Derive. All four quantifier rules appear.

∃x ∀y F(x,y) ⊢ ∀y ∃x F(x,y)
▶Check
1. ∃x ∀y F(x,y) premise 2. │ ∀y F(a,y) assume, a fresh 3. │ F(a,b) 2 ∀E b arbitrary 4. │ ∃x F(x,b) 3 ∃I 5. │ ∀y ∃x F(x,y) 4 ∀I b arbitrary ✓ 6. ∀y ∃x F(x,y) 1, 2–5 ∃E

Line 5 is legal because b is arbitrary — note that a is not arbitrary at that point (it's the assumed witness), which is exactly why the conclusion generalises over y and not over x.

Line 6 is legal because the conclusion contains no a.

13.8 Show the converse of 13.7 is invalid, and say exactly which rule application fails.

∀y ∃x F(x,y) ⊢ ∃x ∀y F(x,y)
▶Check

Counter-model. Domain ℕ, F(x,y) = "x > y". Every y has some larger x, so the premise holds. But no single x exceeds every natural number, so the conclusion fails.

Where a proof attempt breaks: you'd instantiate the premise at an arbitrary b to get ∃x F(x,b), then ∃E gives you a witness a with F(a,b). To reach the goal you now need ∀I on b — but b is no longer arbitrary, because a was chosen after seeing b and the open assumption F(a,b) mentions it.

That failed side condition is the ∀∃ / ∃∀ asymmetry, stated as a rule violation rather than as intuition: the witness was allowed to depend on b, so it can't be lifted out front. Question 4.3 in the first worksheet, from the proof-theoretic side.

Sets 7 and 11 are ordinary derivation practice. Sets 8 and 12 move the needle most — producing a counterexample and diagnosing a broken step are harder than writing a correct proof, and closer to what mid-sem questions ask. Set 10 is the machinery CITS3005 is built on. Set 13 is the one to do slowly: the two side conditions on ∀I and ∃E are the entire difficulty, and 13.8 is where the ∃∀ versus ∀∃ distinction finally becomes mechanical rather than a matter of squinting at it.
