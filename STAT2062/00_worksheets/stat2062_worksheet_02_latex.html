A

Translating English into sets

≈25 min · diagnostic: is this AND or OR?

For every expression you write, find the outcome most likely to break it — usually the both-case or the neither-case — and test it. Over-constraining and under-constraining are the only two ways to be wrong here.

A1

A die is rolled 5 times. Let \(H_j\) be the event that roll \(j\) shows a six.

  1. Exactly one of the five rolls is a six.
  2. The first roll is a six and no later roll is.
  3. At least two rolls are sixes.
  4. The last roll is not a six, but at least one earlier roll is.
  5. At most one of the first three rolls is a six.
Solution
  1. \[\bigcup_{i=1}^{5}\left(H_i\cap\bigcap_{j\ne i}H_j^{c}\right)\] A union of five disjoint cases, one per position of the six.
  2. \(H_1\cap H_2^c\cap H_3^c\cap H_4^c\cap H_5^c\). This is one of the five terms in (a).
  3. \(\bigcup_{i\lt j}\left(H_i\cap H_j\right)\). Equivalently: the complement of "zero or one six."
  4. \(H_5^c\cap(H_1\cup H_2\cup H_3\cup H_4)\).
  5. \[\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{3}H_j^{c}\right)\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\left(H_i\cap\bigcap_{j\le 3,\,j\ne i}H_j^{c}\right)\] Zero sixes, or exactly one. Equivalently \(\bigl(\bigcup_{i\lt j\le3}(H_i\cap H_j)\bigr)^{c}\).
"Exactly one" is never a single intersection. It's a union over which one, and each branch pins every other position to its complement. If your (a) has no union in it, you've described one specific arrangement rather than the event.
A2

Each expression below is a plausible-looking wrong answer to "at least one of \(A,B,C\) fails to occur." For each, name one outcome it mishandles.

  1. \(A^c\cap B^c\cap C^c\)
  2. \((A\cap B\cap C)^c\cup(A\cup B\cup C)\)
  3. \((A^c\cap B^c)\cup(B^c\cap C^c)\)

Then write the correct expression.

Solution

Correct answer: \((A\cap B\cap C)^c=A^c\cup B^c\cup C^c\).

  1. Too strict. If \(A\) occurs but \(B,C\) don't, the event should hold — but this expression rejects it.
  2. Too loose. If all three occur, the first term is false but \(A\cup B\cup C\) is true, so the whole thing wrongly accepts it. The \(\cup\) has swallowed the constraint entirely.
  3. Too strict in a subtler way. If only \(A\) fails — \(B\) and \(C\) both occur — the event should hold, but neither bracket does.
Negate the whole sentence, then simplify. "At least one fails" is the negation of "all three occur," which is \(A\cap B\cap C\). Writing the negation first and De Morgan-ing second is far more reliable than assembling cases.
A3

Classify each sample space as finite, countably infinite, or uncountably infinite, and give \(|S|\) where finite.

  1. Record which of 8 servers are online.
  2. Roll a die repeatedly; record the number of rolls until the running total first exceeds 100.
  3. Record the exact proportion of a 24-hour period a machine was idle.
  4. Record the infinite sequence of daily "up" / "down" statuses of a server, forever.
Solution
  1. Each server is independently up or down: \(|S|=2^8=256\). Finite.
  2. \(S=\{17,18,\dots,101\}\) — at least 17 rolls (all sixes) and at most 101 (all ones). Finite, \(|S|=85\). Worth noticing this one is bounded; the "repeatedly" phrasing invites you to say countably infinite.
  3. \(S=[0,1]\). Uncountable.
  4. The set of infinite binary sequences. Uncountable, by Cantor's diagonal argument.
(b) and (d) are the same experiment shape with different recordings. Record a count and you get something countable — often finite. Record the whole infinite sequence and you jump to uncountable. Always ask what is being written down.
B

Add or multiply

≈30 min · diagnostic: are these alternatives, or stages?

Multiply when both decisions happen in building one object. Add when the object falls into one of several mutually exclusive types. Before writing \(+\) or \(\times\), say the sentence: "first I choose …, then I choose …" (multiply) or "it is either … or …" (add).

B1

A café offers 4 breads, 6 fillings, and 3 sauces.

  1. How many sandwiches with one bread, one filling, and one sauce?
  2. How many with one bread, one filling, and either one sauce or no sauce?
  3. How many with one bread and exactly two different fillings, no sauce?
  4. How many with one bread, exactly two fillings, and exactly one sauce?
Solution
  1. \(4\cdot6\cdot3=72\). Three stages.
  2. \(4\cdot6\cdot4=96\) — the sauce stage now has 4 options, the three sauces plus "none." You could also write \(72+24\), splitting by sauce/no sauce. Same answer; the "none" trick is cleaner.
  3. \(4\cdot\binom{6}{2}=4\cdot15=60\). Two fillings, order irrelevant.
  4. \(4\cdot\binom{6}{2}\cdot3=180\).
\(\binom62\) not \(6\cdot5\). Ham-then-cheese and cheese-then-ham are the same sandwich. Whenever a stage picks several items of the same kind, ask whether their order distinguishes anything real.
B2

A study group has 6 second-years and 4 third-years. A team of 4 is chosen.

  1. How many teams have exactly 1 third-year?
  2. How many have exactly 2 third-years?
  3. How many have at least 1 third-year?
  4. Verify (c) by summing the relevant cases, and say which route you'd use under time pressure.
Solution
  1. \(\binom41\binom63=4\cdot20=80\)
  2. \(\binom42\binom62=6\cdot15=90\)
  3. Complement: \(\binom{10}{4}-\binom64=210-15=195\).
  4. \(80+90+\binom43\binom61+\binom44\binom60=80+90+24+1=195\) ✓. Under pressure, the complement — one subtraction instead of four products.
Multiply within a case, add across cases. Inside "exactly 2 third-years" you pick third-years and second-years, so multiply. Across "exactly 1," "exactly 2," … the team is one or the other, so add. Both operations appear in (d), doing different jobs.
B3

A 6-character ID uses 26 letters and 10 digits, repeats allowed.

  1. How many IDs start with a letter?
  2. How many contain at least one letter and at least one digit?
  3. How many have all six characters distinct?
  4. What is the probability a random ID has no repeated character?
Solution
  1. \(26\cdot36^5=26\cdot60{,}466{,}176=1{,}572{,}120{,}576\)
  2. Total, minus all-letters, minus all-digits: \(36^6-26^6-10^6=2{,}176{,}782{,}336-308{,}915{,}776-1{,}000{,}000=1{,}866{,}866{,}560\).
  3. \(36\cdot35\cdot34\cdot33\cdot32\cdot31=1{,}402{,}410{,}240\), i.e. \(\frac{36!}{30!}\).
  4. \[\frac{1{,}402{,}410{,}240}{2{,}176{,}782{,}336}\approx0.6442\]
(b) subtracts two disjoint bad sets. "No letter" and "no digit" cannot both happen in a 6-character ID, so there is nothing to add back. If the string had length 0 they would overlap — which is exactly the situation inclusion–exclusion exists to handle.
Fencepost check on (c): \(n-k+1=36-6+1=31\), matching the last factor.
C

When to flip to the complement

≈25 min · diagnostic: which side has fewer cases?

The complement is a tool, not a reflex. Flip when the direct count fans out into many cases and the opposite is a single clean condition. Don't flip when it's the other way round.

C1

For each, say whether you would count directly or via the complement, and why. Don't compute yet.

  1. Among 8 coin flips, at least one head.
  2. Among 8 coin flips, exactly four heads.
  3. Among 30 students, at least two born in the same month.
  4. Among 30 students, exactly three born in January.
  5. A 5-digit PIN with at least one repeated digit.
Solution
  1. Complement. "No heads" is a single outcome; "at least one" spans 255.
  2. Direct. \(\binom84=70\). The complement is everything else — far messier.
  3. Complement. "All distinct months" is one clean condition. Note this one is a trick: 30 students, 12 months, so by pigeonhole the probability is exactly 1.
  4. Direct. \(\binom{30}{3}\left(\frac1{12}\right)^3\left(\frac{11}{12}\right)^{27}\). "Exactly" is already one case.
  5. Complement. "All distinct" is \(10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7\cdot6\); the direct count would split by repeat pattern.
The tell is the word. "At least" and "at most" usually flip; "exactly" usually doesn't. But check rather than obey — (c) shows a phrase can be decided by pigeonhole before any counting starts.
C2

A lift starts with 5 passengers and stops at 8 floors. Each passenger picks a floor uniformly at random and independently.

  1. How many ways can the passengers' choices fall out?
  2. What is the probability they all pick different floors?
  3. What is the probability at least two pick the same floor?
  4. What is the probability nobody picks floor 1?
Solution
  1. \(8^5=32{,}768\). Each passenger independently has 8 choices — with replacement, order matters (passengers are distinguishable).
  2. \(\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4}{8^5}=\frac{6720}{32768}\approx0.2051\)
  3. \(1-0.2051\approx0.7949\)
  4. \(\left(\frac78\right)^5\approx0.5129\)
(d) is not a complement problem. "Nobody picks floor 1" is already the single clean condition — each passenger independently avoids one floor. Flipping it to "at least one picks floor 1" would make it harder. Same worksheet, opposite instinct required.
This is the birthday problem with 8 days and 5 people. Same structure, and it's why (c) is so large — 5 people over only 8 floors collide easily.
C3

Twelve identical-looking USB drives sit in a drawer; 3 are faulty. You grab 4 at random.

  1. Probability none is faulty.
  2. Probability at least one is faulty.
  3. Probability exactly one is faulty.
  4. Probability all three faulty ones are among your 4.
Solution

There are \(\binom{12}{4}=495\) equally likely grabs.

  1. \[\frac{\binom{9}{4}}{\binom{12}{4}}=\frac{126}{495}\approx0.2545\]
  2. \[1-\frac{126}{495}=\frac{369}{495}\approx0.7455\]
  3. \[\frac{\binom{3}{1}\binom{9}{3}}{\binom{12}{4}}=\frac{3\cdot84}{495}=\frac{252}{495}\approx0.5091\]
  4. \[\frac{\binom{3}{3}\binom{9}{1}}{\binom{12}{4}}=\frac{9}{495}\approx0.0182\]
Full check: \(126+252+\binom32\binom92+9=126+252+108+9=495\) ✓. The four cases (0, 1, 2, 3 faulty) partition every possible grab, so they must sum to the total.
D

Assigning roles without overcounting

≈35 min · diagnostic: is this role shared or unique?

The hardest habit in this lecture. When several categories are assigned to roles, ask whether the role is held by one category or shared between several. Shared roles are unordered, so they take a \(\binom{n}{k}\), not a falling product.

D1

A tray holds 20 pastries: 5 each of four kinds (almond, berry, custard, date), all individually distinct. You take 5.

  1. How many selections have shape \(2{+}2{+}1\) across three different kinds?
  2. How many have shape \(3{+}1{+}1\)?
  3. How many have shape \(3{+}2\)?
  4. Which of the three is most likely, and why?
Solution

Total: \(\binom{20}{5}=15{,}504\).

  1. Two kinds share the doubled role — \(\binom42=6\) — then one of the remaining two kinds is the single, \(\binom21=2\): \[\begin{aligned} \binom{4}{2}\binom{5}{2}\binom{5}{2}\cdot\binom{2}{1}\binom{5}{1} &=6\cdot10\cdot10\cdot2\cdot5\\[2pt]&=6000. \end{aligned}\]
  2. One kind is tripled \(\binom41=4\); two of the remaining three share the singleton role, \(\binom32=3\): \[\begin{aligned} \binom{4}{1}\binom{5}{3}\cdot\binom{3}{2}\binom{5}{1}\binom{5}{1} &=4\cdot10\cdot3\cdot5\cdot5\\[2pt]&=3000. \end{aligned}\]
  3. Tripled kind and doubled kind are different roles, so order matters between them: \(4\cdot3=12\) ways to assign, i.e. \(\binom41\binom31\): \[\binom{4}{1}\binom{5}{3}\cdot\binom{3}{1}\binom{5}{2}=4\cdot10\cdot3\cdot10=1200.\]
  4. \(2{+}2{+}1\) at \(\approx0.387\), then \(3{+}1{+}1\) at \(\approx0.193\), then \(3{+}2\) at \(\approx0.077\). Spread-out shapes dominate because there are more ways to realise them.
The whole set in one line: in (a) the two doubled kinds are interchangeable, so \(\binom42\). In (c) the tripled kind and doubled kind are not interchangeable — almond-triple-with-berry-double differs from berry-triple-with-almond-double — so \(4\cdot3\). Using \(4\cdot3\) in (a) doubles your answer; using \(\binom42\) in (c) halves it.
D2

A department must fill roles from 9 staff.

  1. How many ways to choose a committee of 3?
  2. How many ways to choose a chair, a secretary, and a treasurer (all different people)?
  3. How many ways to choose a committee of 3 with one of them designated chair?
  4. How many ways to choose a committee of 3 with a chair and a secretary from within it?
Solution
  1. \(\binom93=84\)
  2. \(9\cdot8\cdot7=504\), or \(\binom93\cdot3!=84\cdot6\). Three distinct roles, so all orderings count.
  3. \(\binom93\cdot3=252\). Cross-check: \(9\cdot\binom82=9\cdot28=252\) ✓
  4. \(\binom93\cdot3\cdot2=504\) — same as (b), as it must be, since naming a chair, a secretary and one unlabelled third member is the same as naming three distinct roles.
Roles that are named are ordered; roles that aren't, aren't. (a) has no roles: \(\binom93\). (b) has three named roles: multiply by \(3!\). (c) names one: multiply by 3. The factor you tack on is exactly the number of ways to hand out the labels.
D3

Eight people sit in a row of 8 chairs, all orderings equally likely. Three of them are P, Q, R.

  1. How many arrangements have P, Q, R all sitting together as a block?
  2. What is the probability of that?
  3. What is the probability P and Q are adjacent but R is not adjacent to either?
  4. What is the probability P sits somewhere to the left of Q, who sits somewhere to the left of R?
Solution
  1. Handcuff all three: 6 objects, \(6!\) arrangements, times \(3!\) internal orders. \(6!\cdot3!=720\cdot6=4320\).
  2. \(\frac{4320}{8!}=\frac{4320}{40320}=\frac{3}{28}\approx0.107\)
  3. Handcuff P and Q into a block (2 internal orders). Now arrange the block, R, and the other 5 — but R must not touch the block. Place the 5 others: \(5!\) ways, creating 6 gaps. Choose 2 different gaps for the block and R: \(6\cdot5=30\) ordered ways. Total \(5!\cdot2\cdot30=120\cdot2\cdot30=7200\), so \(\frac{7200}{40320}=\frac{5}{28}\approx0.179\).
  4. \(\frac{1}{3!}=\frac16\). All \(3!\) relative orders of P, Q, R are equally likely by symmetry, and exactly one is the required one.
The gap method in (c). When something must not be adjacent, seat everyone else first, then slot the constrained items into the gaps between them. Trying to count non-adjacency directly is where this problem eats people.
(d) generalises B3(d) from Worksheet 1: for \(m\) specified people, the probability of any one particular relative order is \(1/m!\), regardless of how many others are present.
E

Story proofs

≈35 min · do while sharp

Each of these has a concrete version you have already computed. Find it first, then strip the numbers out. "Both sides count the number of ways to …" — finish that sentence before writing anything.

E1

Give a story proof of Pascal's rule: for \(1\le k\le n-1\), \[\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}.\]

Solution

Both sides count committees of size \(k\) from \(n\) people. Single out one person, call her Ada.

Right. Every committee either contains Ada or doesn't — mutually exclusive, exhaustive. If it does, the other \(k-1\) members come from the remaining \(n-1\): \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\). If it doesn't, all \(k\) come from the remaining \(n-1\): \(\binom{n-1}{k}\). Add the two cases.

Left. Count them all at once. \(\blacksquare\)

The addition here is legitimate precisely because the two cases can't both happen — the same add-versus-multiply test from Set B, now inside a proof.
E2

Give a story proof that \[\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{j}=\binom{n}{j}\binom{n-j}{k},\qquad j+k\le n.\]

Solution

Both sides count ways to split \(n\) people into a red team of size \(k\), a blue team of size \(j\), and everyone else left over.

Left. Pick the red team from all \(n\), then the blue team from the \(n-k\) who remain.

Right. Pick blue first, then red from the \(n-j\) who remain.

The teams are differently labelled, so no ordering issue arises — the two routes just differ in which colour is chosen first. \(\blacksquare\)

This is D2 of this worksheet in general form: choosing chair-then-committee versus committee-then-chair.
E3

Give a story proof that \[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}2^{k}=3^{n}.\]

Hint: three outcomes per person.

Solution

Both sides count the ways to assign each of \(n\) people to exactly one of three groups — say in-person, online, or absent.

Right. Three independent choices per person: \(3^n\).

Left. First decide who is not absent — if that group has size \(k\), there are \(\binom{n}{k}\) ways to choose it. Then each of those \(k\) people is independently in-person or online: \(2^k\). Sum over \(k\). \(\blacksquare\)

Check at \(n=2\): \(1+4+4=9=3^2\). This generalises D1 of Worksheet 1, which is the \(m=2\) case — and the same argument gives \(\sum_k\binom nk m^k=(m+1)^n\).
E4 · stretch

Give a story proof that the number of ways to split \(2n\) people into \(n\) unlabelled pairs is \[\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}\,n!}.\]

Solution

Line all \(2n\) people up: \((2n)!\) orderings. Read the lineup as pairs — positions 1–2, 3–4, and so on.

Every pairing arises this way, but repeatedly. Swapping the two people within a pair leaves the pairing unchanged: \(2^n\) such swaps. Permuting the \(n\) pairs among themselves also leaves it unchanged: \(n!\) such permutations. So each pairing is produced exactly \(2^n\cdot n!\) times.

Adjusting for overcounting gives \(\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Check at \(n=2\): \(\frac{24}{4\cdot2}=3\), and indeed {AB,CD}, {AC,BD}, {AD,BC}. Note the two divisions are doing exactly the jobs \(r!\) and \((n-r)!\) do inside \(\binom nr\) — kill an order you invented but don't care about.
F

Axioms, bounds, and mixed practice

≈35 min · exam-shaped

The last set mixes types without telling you which tool applies — closer to what a quiz feels like than the topic-sorted sets above.

F1

Given \(\Prob(A)=0.55\), \(\Prob(B)=0.35\), \(\Prob(A\cup B)=0.70\).

  1. Find \(\Prob(A\cap B)\).
  2. Find \(\Prob(A^c\cap B)\) and \(\Prob(A\cap B^c)\).
  3. Find the probability that exactly one occurs.
  4. Could \(A\) and \(B\) be mutually exclusive? Could \(B\subseteq A\)?
Solution
  1. \(0.55+0.35-0.70=0.20\)
  2. \(\Prob(A^c\cap B)=0.35-0.20=0.15\); \(\Prob(A\cap B^c)=0.55-0.20=0.35\)
  3. \(0.15+0.35=0.50\)
  4. No to both. Mutually exclusive would force \(\Prob(A\cap B)=0\), but it's \(0.20\). \(B\subseteq A\) would force \(\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(B)=0.35\), but it's \(0.20\).
F2

In a survey of 500 people: 260 use a bike, 180 use a bus, 150 drive; 70 use bike and bus, 60 bike and car, 50 bus and car; 20 use all three.

  1. How many use at least one?
  2. How many use none?
  3. How many use exactly one?
  4. How many use bike or bus but not a car?
Solution
  1. \(260+180+150-70-60-50+20=430\)
  2. \(500-430=70\)
  3. \(S_1-2S_2+3S_3=590-360+60=290\)
  4. \(\Prob(\text{bike}\cup\text{bus})-\Prob(\text{bike}\cup\text{bus})\cap\text{car}\). Bike or bus: \(260+180-70=370\). Of those, the car users are \(60+50-20=90\). So \(370-90=280\).
Check: exactly one 290, exactly two \(=S_2-3S_3=180-60=120\), all three 20. \(290+120+20=430\) ✓
F3

A pipeline has 12 stages. Each stage independently succeeds with probability \(0.995\).

  1. Use the union bound to give a guaranteed lower bound on the probability all 12 succeed.
  2. Compute the exact probability, assuming independence.
  3. Why is the bound in (a) worth having if (b) is computable?
  4. How many stages before the guaranteed bound drops below \(0.90\)?
Solution
  1. \(1-12(0.005)=0.94\)
  2. \(0.995^{12}\approx0.9416\)
  3. The bound needs no independence assumption. If stages share a failure cause — one machine, one power supply — the exact calculation in (b) is simply wrong, while the bound still holds.
  4. Need \(1-0.005n\ge0.90\), so \(n\le20\). At \(n=21\) the guarantee breaks.
Here the bound (0.94) and the truth (0.9416) are close, because the failure probabilities are tiny. With larger per-item failure rates the union bound degrades fast and can go vacuous — as in Worksheet 1's F3.
F4

Six distinct books are placed on a shelf at random.

  1. Probability that a particular book is at one of the two ends.
  2. Probability that two particular books are at the two ends.
  3. Probability that no book sits in the position matching its own number, if the books are numbered 1–6.
  4. Compare (c) with \(1/e\).
Solution
  1. \(\frac{2}{6}=\frac13\) — it is equally likely to be in any of 6 positions, 2 of which are ends.
  2. \(\frac{2\cdot4!}{6!}=\frac{48}{720}=\frac1{15}\)
  3. By inclusion–exclusion, \[\begin{aligned} \Prob(\text{no match})&=\sum_{k=0}^{6}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\\[2pt] &=1-1+\tfrac12-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{24}-\tfrac1{120}+\tfrac1{720}\\[2pt] &=\tfrac{265}{720}\approx0.3681 \end{aligned}\]
  4. \(1/e\approx0.3679\) — already correct to three decimal places at \(n=6\).
(c) is the complement of de Montmort's matching problem. The count 265 is the number of derangements of 6 objects.

The six diagnostics, in one place. Is this AND or OR? Are these alternatives or stages? Which side has fewer cases? Is this role shared or unique? What is actually being recorded? What can I check the answer against?

If a set goes badly, the matching set in Worksheet 1 is the gentler version — A↔A, B↔B/C, C↔B, D↔C, E↔D, F↔E/F.
