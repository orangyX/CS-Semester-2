Part A · Normal forms

0 / 5 · ~35 min

Slides 4–7. The one idea to hold onto: a normal form removes redundancy by eliminating some connectives and restricting where the rest may appear — and each of the three forms restricts a different thing. A2 is the lecture's own classification exercise and A5 is the observation on slide 11 that most people read past.

A1recall

Define negation normal form, conjunctive normal form and disjunctive normal form. Then write the BNF that specifies CNF, and say which of the three forms the other two are special cases of.

Definitions
formcondition
NNFthe only connectives are $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\neg$, and $\neg$ is applied only to atomic formulas
CNFof the form $A_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge A_m$, where each $A_i$ is a disjunction of one or more literals
DNFof the form $A_1 \vee \cdots \vee A_m$, where each $A_i$ is a conjunction of one or more literals
The BNF

With $L$ any atomic formula:

$$ \texttt{lit} ::= L \mid \neg L \mid \top \mid \bot \qquad \texttt{disj} ::= \texttt{lit} \mid \texttt{disj} \vee \texttt{lit} \qquad \texttt{conj} ::= \texttt{disj} \mid \texttt{conj} \wedge \texttt{disj} $$

Read it bottom up: literals build disjunctions, disjunctions build conjunctions, and nothing goes the other way. That one-way structure is what "normal" means — there is no production letting a $\wedge$ appear inside a $\vee$.

Checks

CNF and DNF are both special cases of NNF. Both admit only $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\neg$-on-atoms, so anything in CNF or DNF is automatically in NNF. The converse fails — A3 has the counterexample. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: slide 5's reason for normal forms is mechanisation. Every extra connective is another case in every procedure, so reasoning is easier over a language with fewer of them. The price is that the formula gets longer, sometimes a great deal longer — B4.

A5prove

Slide 11 remarks that if a formula is valid, its CNF should reduce to $\top$. Explain why, then demonstrate it on $P \vee \neg P$ and on $(P \to Q) \vee (Q \to P)$.

Step 4 removes a disjunction containing both $P$ and $\neg P$. What must a valid CNF formula look like, clause by clause?

Why

A formula in CNF is a conjunction $A_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge A_m$, so it is valid exactly when every $A_i$ is valid. Each $A_i$ is a disjunction of literals, and a disjunction of literals is valid exactly when it contains some atom together with its negation — otherwise you can falsify it by making every literal in it false, which is always possible because the literals in a clause without a complementary pair impose no conflicting demands.

Step 4 deletes precisely those clauses, since they are $\top$. So a valid formula loses every clause and what remains is the empty conjunction, $\top$. ∎

$P \vee \neg P$

Already in CNF, one clause. It contains $P$ and $\neg P$, so step 4 removes it, leaving $\top$. ✓

$(P \to Q) \vee (Q \to P)$

Step 1, eliminate $\to$:   $(\neg P \vee Q) \vee (\neg Q \vee P)$

Steps 2 and 3: nothing to do — the negations are already on atoms, and there is no $\wedge$ to push a $\vee$ past. Flattening the associativity gives one clause, $\neg P \vee Q \vee \neg Q \vee P$.

Step 4: the clause contains $Q$ and $\neg Q$ — and separately $P$ and $\neg P$ — so it is $\top$. Nothing remains. ✓

Checks

Independent check on the second: it says "either $P$ implies $Q$ or $Q$ implies $P$", which is true under all four valuations, since at most one of $P$, $Q$ can be true-without-the-other. Valid, as the CNF reported. ✓

Why this matters

How to use this: this is a free correctness check on any conversion. If you were converting something you believe valid and the clauses do not all vanish, you have made a mistake — and if you were converting something you believe satisfiable but not valid and everything vanishes, likewise. It also connects forward: a valid formula reduces to nothing, and a contradiction reduces to $\square$, which is what all of Part E is built on.

B1compute

State the four steps of slide 7, then apply them to the lecture's own example, $(P \vee Q) \to (Q \vee R)$, showing the formula after each step.

The four steps
1Eliminate $\to$ and $\leftrightarrow$
2Push all the negations down to the literals
3Move all the conjunctions to the outside — push disjunctions in until they apply only to literals
4Remove the remaining $\top$ and $\bot$ constants, and any clause containing both $L$ and $\neg L$
The conversion

Start:   $(P \vee Q) \to (Q \vee R)$

Step 1, by $\alpha \to \beta \equiv \neg\alpha \vee \beta$:   $\neg(P \vee Q) \vee (Q \vee R)$

Step 2, by De Morgan:   $(\neg P \wedge \neg Q) \vee (Q \vee R)$

Step 3, distributing the $\vee$ over the $\wedge$:   $(\neg P \vee Q \vee R) \wedge (\neg Q \vee Q \vee R)$

Step 4: the second clause contains $Q$ and $\neg Q$, so it is $\top$ and drops out:   $\neg P \vee Q \vee R$

One clause: $\{\neg P, Q, R\}$.

Checks

Read the answer back as an implication: $\neg P \vee Q \vee R \equiv P \to (Q \vee R)$. So the conversion has quietly discovered that the $Q$ in the antecedent was redundant — if $Q$ holds then $Q \vee R$ holds anyway. Confirm on a truth table: the original and $P \to (Q \vee R)$ agree on all eight valuations. ✓

Slide 5's point, made concrete: step 2 is guaranteed to terminate because each equivalence it uses moves the negation strictly inward, so the negated subformula gets smaller every time. There is no way to loop. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: step 4 is not cosmetic. Dropping tautologous clauses is what lets a valid formula vanish entirely (A5), and it keeps the clause set small enough to resolve on.

Slide 23's example. To prove $P \wedge Q \to Q \wedge P$ by resolution you first negate it and convert. Do the conversion and give the clause set. Then say why negating comes before converting rather than after.

The conversion

Negate:   $\neg\bigl(P \wedge Q \to Q \wedge P\bigr)$

Step 1:   $\neg\bigl(\neg(P \wedge Q) \vee (Q \wedge P)\bigr)$

Step 2, De Morgan:   $(P \wedge Q) \wedge \neg(Q \wedge P)$, then $(P \wedge Q) \wedge (\neg Q \vee \neg P)$

Step 3: already CNF — a conjunction of $P$, $Q$ and $(\neg Q \vee \neg P)$.

Clauses:   $\{P\}$,   $\{Q\}$,   $\{\neg Q, \neg P\}$

Why negate first

Because resolution is a refutation procedure: it derives $\square$ from an unsatisfiable set, and the thing you want to be unsatisfiable is $\neg\alpha$. Converting $\alpha$ first and then negating would leave you negating a conjunction of clauses, which is not in clause form and would need converting all over again.

There is also a practical reason. Negating a valid formula turns it into a contradiction, and contradictions have short clause sets — here, three clauses from a formula that would itself have converted to $\top$ and given you nothing to work with (A5).

Checks

Verify the set really is unsatisfiable before resolving: $\{P\}$ forces $P$ true, $\{Q\}$ forces $Q$ true, and then $\{\neg Q, \neg P\}$ has both literals false. No valuation survives. ✓ E2 derives $\square$ from exactly these three.

Why this matters

The moral: the four-step procedure and resolution are two halves of one method. Slide 21 states it as four numbered steps — negate, convert, resolve, conclude — and Part B is the second of them.

Slide 15's example. Skolemise $\exists X \forall Y \exists Z.\ R(X,Y,Z)$, one existential at a time, saying which rule you are using and why each Skolem term has the arguments it does.

Then Skolemise $\forall Y \exists X \forall W \exists Z.\ S(X,Y,W,Z)$.

The rule

For $\forall t_1 \dots \forall t_n. \exists X\, \alpha(t_1,\dots,t_n,X)$, the value of $X$ depends on $t_1,\dots,t_n$, so introduce a new function symbol to represent that dependency and drop the quantifier. With no universals in scope, use a Skolem constant. Slide 15: always start with the left-most existential quantifier.

First example

Start:   $\exists X \forall Y \exists Z.\ R(X,Y,Z)$

Left-most existential is $\exists X$, and no universal is in scope — nothing stands to its left. So $X$ becomes a Skolem constant $a$:

$\forall Y \exists Z.\ R(a,Y,Z)$

Next existential is $\exists Z$, and it sits inside $\forall Y$, so $Z$ depends on $Y$ and becomes a one-place Skolem function $f$:

$\forall Y.\ R(a,Y,f(Y))$

Second example

$\exists X$ is inside $\forall Y$ only, so $X \mapsto g(Y)$. Then $\exists Z$ is inside both $\forall Y$ and $\forall W$, so $Z \mapsto h(Y,W)$:

$$ \forall Y \forall W.\ S\bigl(g(Y),\, Y,\, W,\, h(Y,W)\bigr) $$ Checks

Count the arguments, do not guess them. Each Skolem term takes exactly the universally quantified variables to its left. In the second example $g$ takes one and $h$ takes two, and $h$ takes $Y$ as well as $W$ even though $W$ is nearer — everything to the left counts. ✓

Why left to right: if you Skolemised $\exists Z$ first you would correctly get $h(Y,W)$, but then Skolemising $\exists X$ afterwards you might forget that $h$ already mentions $Y$. Left to right keeps the dependencies straight without bookkeeping. ✓

Each Skolem symbol must be fresh — not used elsewhere in the formula or the clause set. Reusing $f$ for two different existentials silently identifies two unrelated witnesses. ✓

C4prove

Slide 15 says Skolemisation is not an axiom, does not preserve validity, but does preserve satisfiability — and offers $\exists X.\bigl(P(a) \to P(X)\bigr)$ to think about. Work it out.

  1. Is the original valid? Prove your answer.
  2. Skolemise it. Is the result valid?
  3. What does this show, and why is it enough for resolution?

For (a), you get to choose the witness, and one particular choice makes the implication trivially true. For (b), the Skolem constant is a new symbol, so nothing constrains what it denotes.

(a) The original is valid

Take any interpretation $I$. Domains are non-empty, and $a^{I} \in D^{I}$. Choose the witness $X = a$. Then the body is $P(a) \to P(a)$, which holds in every interpretation. So $\exists X.(P(a) \to P(X))$ is satisfied by $I$. Since $I$ was arbitrary, the sentence is valid. ∎

It looks like it should say something — "there is something such that if $a$ is $P$, so is it" — and it says nothing, because you may always take that something to be $a$ itself.

(b) The Skolemisation is not valid

The $\exists X$ has no universal in scope, so it becomes a fresh constant $c$:

$$ P(a) \to P(c) $$

Not valid. Counterexample: $D^{I} = \{1,2\}$, $a^{I} = 1$, $c^{I} = 2$, $P^{I}(1) = \top$, $P^{I}(2) = \bot$. Then the antecedent holds and the consequent fails, so the implication is false. ∎

This step is exactly where the freshness of $c$ is used: because $c$ is a new symbol, an interpretation is free to send it anywhere, including somewhere $P$ fails.

(c) What it shows

A valid formula has become an invalid one, so Skolemisation does not preserve validity, and is therefore not an axiom. What it does preserve is satisfiability: $P(a) \to P(c)$ is still satisfiable — send $c$ to $a^{I}$, or make $P^{I}$ true everywhere.

Slide 15's justification is that although the transformation assumes the function exists, given any interpretation you can always add such a function. If the original is satisfiable there is a witness for each combination of universally quantified values, and defining the Skolem function to pick those witnesses extends the interpretation without disturbing anything else.

That is enough for resolution because resolution asks only one question: is this clause set unsatisfiable? Satisfiability is preserved in both directions, so the answer is the same before and after.

Checks

Confirm the direction of the loss. Skolemisation makes formulas logically stronger — it names a witness that was merely claimed to exist — so a satisfiable formula stays satisfiable but a valid one can stop being valid. It can never turn an unsatisfiable formula into a satisfiable one, which is the direction that would break refutation. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: this is why you must negate the goal and hunt for a contradiction rather than converting the goal and trying to derive it. Skolemisation is only safe inside a satisfiability question.

Slides 24–29. The one idea to hold onto: unification computes a substitution from two terms, rather than your choosing one — which is the whole reason resolution automates and natural deduction does not. D1recall

Define substitution, what it means to unify two predicate statements, and most general unifier. Give the notation slide 25 uses for applying a substitution.

Definitions

A substitution $\theta : \texttt{var} \to \texttt{terms}$ assigns a term to each variable.

Given a predicate $p(t_1, \dots, t_{\#p})$, the notation $p(t_1,\dots,t_{\#p})\backslash\theta$ is the result of replacing every variable $X$ appearing in any of the terms by $\theta(X)$. Slide 25's example:

$$ \texttt{drive}(\texttt{father}(X), Y)\backslash[X \mapsto \texttt{sam},\ Y \mapsto \texttt{morris}] \;=\; \texttt{drive}(\texttt{father}(\texttt{sam}), \texttt{morris}) $$

To unify two predicate statements $p_1, p_2$ with the same predicate symbol is to find substitutions $\theta_1, \theta_2$ such that $p_1\backslash\theta_1$ and $p_2\backslash\theta_2$ are identical.

The most general unifier is the pair of substitutions that makes the predicates equal with the least commitment.

Checks

Note the definition uses two substitutions, one per predicate, rather than one applied to both. That matters when the same variable name occurs in both — slide 25's own example unifies $\texttt{drive}(\texttt{father}(X),Y)$ with $\texttt{drive}(X, \texttt{car}(X))$, where a single substitution could not work because $X$ would have to be both $\texttt{father}(\texttt{sam})$ and $\texttt{sam}$. The usual alternative is to rename the clauses apart first and use one substitution, which is what slide 30 suggests at resolution time. ✓

Why this matters

How to use this: "least commitment" is the operative phrase in D3. A unifier that binds more variables than it has to still unifies — it is just not the most general one, and using it throws away solutions.

Answer

Yes, resolution applies — but only after unifying. The complementary literals are $Q(g(a))$ and $\neg Q(X)$, and they are not syntactically complementary as they stand: one mentions $g(a)$ and the other a variable. The substitution

$$ X \mapsto g(a) $$

$$ \dfrac{\{P,\ Q(g(a))\} \qquad \{R,\ \neg Q(X)\}}{\{P,\ R\}}\;{\small(\text{res},\ X \mapsto g(a))} $$ Do you get $\square$?

No. The resolvent is $\{P, R\}$, which has two literals left. The empty clause requires both parent clauses to have nothing besides the resolved pair — that is, resolving $\{L\}$ with $\{\neg L\}$.

These two clauses are perfectly satisfiable together: make $P$ true and $Q^{I}$ false everywhere, and both hold. So no refutation should be derivable from them alone, and none is. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: this is why slides 24 to 29 exist. Propositional resolution needs the two literals to be literally complementary; full clausal logic needs a way to make them so, and that way is unification.

Slide 26's question. For the terms $f(X,Y)$ and $f(a,Z)$, which of these is the more general unifier, and why?

$$ \theta_1 = [X \mapsto a,\ Y \mapsto Z] \qquad\qquad \theta_2 = [X \mapsto a,\ Y \mapsto a,\ Z \mapsto a] $$
Answer

$\theta_1$ is the most general unifier. Both work — under $\theta_1$ both terms become $f(a,Z)$, and under $\theta_2$ both become $f(a,a)$ — but $\theta_2$ commits to more than it needs to.

The test: $\theta_2$ can be obtained from $\theta_1$ by applying a further substitution, namely $[Z \mapsto a]$, but not the other way round. Anything $\theta_2$ unifies, $\theta_1$ unifies too, and more besides.

Why the difference costs you

$\theta_1$ leaves $Z$ free, so the resolvent it produces still holds for every value of $Z$. $\theta_2$ pins $Z$ to $a$, so any conclusion drawn afterwards is only about $a$ — every other solution has been thrown away before you looked for it.

Resolution is still sound with a non-general unifier; it stops being complete. You can fail to find a refutation that exists, because you narrowed too early.

Checks

Verify $\theta_1$ really unifies: $f(X,Y)\backslash\theta_1 = f(a,Z)$ and $f(a,Z)\backslash\theta_1 = f(a,Z)$. Identical. ✓ Verified in SWI-Prolog: unifying f(X,Y) with f(a,Z) binds X = a and leaves Y and Z sharing a single unbound variable — exactly $\theta_1$, and no more. ✓

Why this matters

How to use this: when writing a unifier by hand, bind a variable only when forced. If two variables meet, bind one to the other rather than both to a constant — $[Y \mapsto Z]$, not $[Y \mapsto a, Z \mapsto a]$.

E1recall

State the propositional resolution rule and slide 21's four-step procedure for proving a formula $\alpha$. What does the empty clause mean, and how is it written?

The rule $$ \dfrac{L \vee \alpha \qquad \neg L \vee \beta}{\alpha \vee \beta}\;{\small(\text{res})} $$

$L$ is the literal being resolved on; $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are in CNF with no literals in common.

The procedure
  1. Negate the formula — this is the start of a proof by contradiction.
  2. Translate $\neg\alpha$ into CNF and separate out the clauses.
  3. Repeatedly apply the resolution rule, producing new clauses.
  4. If a contradiction is reached, $\neg\alpha$ is refuted, so $\alpha$ is true.
The empty clause

Written $\square$, or as :- in Prolog notation. It is a disjunction of no literals, so nothing satisfies it — deriving it shows the clause set is unsatisfiable.

Why this matters

How to use this: resolution "aims to find a refutation", in slide 21's words. You never build the conclusion. That converts an open-ended search for a proof into a search for one specific object, which is what makes it mechanisable.

E3prove

Slide 22 makes four claims about propositional resolution. State each precisely, and explain the third given the second.

  1. Resolution is sound.
  2. Resolution is not complete.
  3. Resolution is refutation complete.
  4. Propositional clausal logic is decidable.
(a) Sound

If $C_1$ and $C_2$ are clauses and $C_3$ results from applying resolution to them, then for every program $P$ with $P \models C_1$ and $P \models C_2$, we have $P \models C_3$. Resolution never produces a clause that was not already entailed.

(b) Not complete

Given a program $P$, resolution cannot derive every clause that is true in every model of $P$. Slide 22's example: h1 :- h1 is true in every model — it is a tautology, $\neg h_1 \vee h_1$ — but the clause cannot always be derived.

(c) Refutation complete

If a clause set is unsatisfiable, resolution can derive $\square$ from it.

These are consistent because they answer different questions. (b) is about generating consequences: there are entailed clauses resolution will never write down, and tautologies are the obvious family, since resolution only ever combines two clauses you already have and cannot invent $\neg h_1 \vee h_1$ from nothing. (c) is about detecting inconsistency, and there resolution misses nothing.

This step is exactly where the choice to work by refutation is used. Because (b) holds and (c) holds, the only sound way to use resolution as a proof method is to negate the goal — which is why slide 21's procedure begins by negating.

(d) Decidable

The number of atoms in a program $P$ is finite, so there are only finitely many distinct clauses over them. Resolution is refutation complete, so applying it systematically must terminate: either you find a refutation, or you exhaust the possibilities and find no new clauses — and by (c), no refutation exists. Either way you get an answer. ∎

Checks

Note the semantics underneath all four is Herbrand interpretations, which slide 22 leaves as optional reading. You do not need them for the homework, but the results above are stated relative to that semantics. ✓

Contrast E7: (d) fails for full clausal logic, and the reason is exactly the finiteness used here. ✓

The rule

To resolve two clauses you must first find a substitution $\theta$ that makes the two complementary literals the same; propositional resolution then applies, and the substitution is applied to the resulting clause. Resolution "can quickly start to produce expanding clauses". Factoring produces an additional clause with a substitution already applied, collapsing two literals of the same clause into one when they unify.

  1. Negate the formula.
  2. Translate into CNF and separate out the clauses.
  3. Check for obvious factors that may be useful for the next step.
  4. Find a substitution allowing the resolution rule to be applied between two clauses.
  5. Apply resolution using that substitution.
  6. Repeat 3–5 as necessary.
  7. If a contradiction is reached, $\alpha$ is true.

That is why slide 35 can say an execution is "an attempted proof of the refutation of the query". You never negate anything by hand in Prolog — the query notation already is the negation. ✓

Note every step keeps exactly one resolvent and resolves it against a program clause, never two program clauses against each other. That constraint has a name — linear resolution — and it is the L of SLD. ✓

Note also that the answer is not the refutation but the composed substitution. The refutation shows that someone is liked; the substitution says who. ✓

E7prove

Slide 34 says resolution for full clausal logic is refutation complete but only semi-decidable. Explain what breaks relative to E3(d), and say exactly what a machine can and cannot do.

What breaks

E3(d)'s argument used the fact that a propositional program has finitely many atoms, hence finitely many clauses, so systematic resolution must eventually run out of new clauses.

Full clausal logic has function symbols. From a constant $a$ and a unary $f$ you already get $a$, $f(a)$, $f(f(a))$, and so on without end — terms can be infinite in number, so there are infinitely many distinct clauses and the search need never exhaust itself. ∎

Can and cannot
Canfind a refutation whenever one exists. Refutation completeness is unaffected — any inconsistent program can be shown to have a refutation.
Cannotdetermine that a program is consistent. There is no definite procedure for that: you may search forever without distinguishing "no refutation exists" from "not found yet".

That asymmetry is what semi-decidable names — the yes-instances are recognisable, the no-instances are not.

Checks

Compare with Lecture 3, slide 18: validity in first order logic is undecidable but semi-decidable, for the same underlying reason. Resolution has not made the problem harder or easier — it has given a far more mechanisable procedure for a problem whose difficulty was already fixed. ✓

The semantics for full clausal logic is again Herbrand interpretations, which is why the soundness and refutation-completeness results carry over from E3 unchanged; only the decidability one does not. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: this is why Prolog can hang. A query with no answer may loop forever rather than returning false, and no amount of engineering removes that — it is semi-decidability showing up at the prompt. Part F's SLD trees are where you can see which queries do it.

F2compute

State slide 36's execution algorithm in your own words — all seven points. Then say which step is the backtracking step, and which single word in the algorithm makes the search depth-first.

The algorithm
  1. Take the left-most element of the query conjuncts; call it q.
  2. Go through the knowledge base row by row until finding a clause whose head unifies with q.
  3. Apply unification to both the matched clause and the entire query conjunct, then apply resolution, and insert the resulting literals at the front of the query conjunct.
  4. Take the element now at the front and repeat.
  5. If no clause matches q, reverse the last unification and resolution step and continue searching top down for the next match.
  6. If no clause matches and there are no steps left to reverse: no.
  7. If all query conjuncts resolve away: yes, and return the successful substitution.
Backtracking and depth

Step 5 is backtracking — undoing the most recent choice and trying the next clause from where it left off.

The word that makes it depth-first is "front" in step 3. New subgoals are pushed to the front of the conjunct and step 1 always takes the left-most, so the engine drives all the way down one branch before touching anything it postponed. Append them at the back instead and you would get breadth-first search.

Checks

Two orderings are doing separate jobs, and confusing them is the usual source of trouble. Left-most goal in step 1 decides which subgoal to work on; row by row in step 2 decides which clause to try for it. Reordering goals in a body changes the first; reordering clauses in a file changes the second. ✓

Note step 5 says "continue searching top down for the next match", not "start again" — the engine remembers how far down the file it had reached, which is what makes backtracking finite rather than looping over the same clause. ✓

F3prove

SLD resolution stands for three things. Name each, state what it constrains, and say what is bought by it.

S — Selection rule

A rule for finding the next clause and literal to resolve. In Prolog: the left-most literal of the query or resolvent; the first clause in the program whose head unifies with it; and the tail of that clause, after substitution, is inserted at the front of the resolvent.

Bought: no search over which literal to resolve, and no search over which clause. Both choices are made by position, so they cost nothing.

L — Linear resolution

The process always produces linear proofs, so only a single resolvent need be kept track of.

Bought: memory. General resolution accumulates a growing pool of derived clauses, any pair of which might be resolved next; linear resolution keeps exactly one. E6 is linear — every step resolves the current resolvent against a program clause, never two program clauses against each other.

D — Definite clauses

Exactly one positive literal per clause.

Bought: a goal always matches against a unique head, so the engine never reasons by cases about which disjunct of a head holds. This step is exactly where the definite restriction is used — indefinite clauses would force a case split at every step and destroy linearity.

And when nothing matches

Backtrack, to find the next clause from the top of the program that might apply.

Checks

Slide 37's summary: these constraints "make it possible to design very efficient computation engines for Prolog". Each of the three removes one dimension of search — which literal, which stored clause, which disjunct — leaving only one genuine choice point: which program clause to try, resolved by file order and undone by backtracking. ✓

Note what is not bought: completeness of the search. SLD is refutation complete as a calculus, but Prolog's fixed depth-first selection can loop before finding an existing refutation — F5. The restrictions are about efficiency, not correctness. ✓

Slide 38's example. Draw the SLD tree for ?- student_of(S, peter). against

Resolve the left-most goal first. After the first step you have two goals; the branching happens at whichever one the selection rule picks next.

The tree

The root resolves against the only student_of clause, with $T \mapsto peter$:

Left-most is now follows(S,C), and only one fact matches, binding $S \mapsto maria$, $C \mapsto ai\_techniques$:

Slide 38 draws the tree the other way round, selecting teaches(peter,C) first, and then the branching is visible — three children, one per teaches fact:

branchthenoutcome

Both selections give the same answer, S = maria, and different amounts of work — one branch versus three. That is the point of drawing the tree: the set of solutions is fixed by the program, but the shape of the search is fixed by the selection rule, and Prolog's is left-most. ✓

Checks

The declarative reading of the second program is correct. "X is an ancestor of Z if X is an ancestor of some Y who is a parent of Z" is true, and the clause set entails the right things. The failure is entirely in the search order, which is F3's warning about SLD being an efficiency restriction rather than a correctness one. ✓

Doing only the first leaves the loop reachable on backtracking. ✓

Say what a cut does, then trace ?- parent(john, C). with and without it.

What a cut does

It prunes the remaining search. Once execution passes a !, the alternatives that were still open for the goals to its left — and for the clause it appears in — are discarded, so backtracking cannot revisit them.

The trace

Without the cut: parent(john,C) matches clause 1, father(john,C) succeeds with C = paul. Asked for more, Prolog backtracks into clause 2 and tries mother(john,C), which fails — John is not a mother. So one solution.

With the cut: the same first solution, and the cut discards clause 2 before it is ever tried.

querywith cutwithout cut
parent(john,C)[paul][paul]

Verified in SWI-Prolog. Identical.

Checks

So for this query the cut removes only a branch that was going to fail anyway — it saves work and changes nothing. That is the definition of a green cut (G3), and it is why slide 40 says "backtracking obviously fails". ✓

But the colour of a cut is not a property of the cut alone. G2 asks the same program a different question. ✓

Slide 42 defines negation using a cut, and asks what ?- supp(X,Y). returns.

not(P) :- P, !, fail.   not(P).

For (b): when not(pass(X,Y)) is called, are X and Y bound to anything yet?

(a)

not(P) takes a predicate as its argument. If P can be satisfied, the cut fires — preventing backtracking into the second clause — and then fail makes the whole call fail. If P cannot be satisfied, the first clause fails before reaching the cut, so the second clause is tried and succeeds unconditionally.

This is a red cut used deliberately: it removes the second clause's success branch, and that removal is the entire mechanism. It is not classical negation, and is better described as "failure to prove".

(b) The answer

?- supp(X,Y). returns nothing. Verified in SWI-Prolog: no solutions.

Proof. The left-most goal is not(pass(X,Y)), and at that point X and Y are unbound — sat(X,Y) has not run yet. So the call asks whether pass(X,Y) can be satisfied for some substitution. It can: pass(jane,cits3005). So the first not clause succeeds up to the cut and then fails, and the cut blocks the second clause. Hence not(pass(X,Y)) fails, and with the left-most goal failing, supp(X,Y) has no solutions at all. ∎

Verified directly: not(pass(X,Y)) with both unbound is false.

(c) The fix

Swap the goals.

supp(X,Y) :- sat(X,Y), not(pass(X,Y)).

Now sat binds X and Y first, so the negation is asked about a ground goal — "did this person pass this unit?" Verified: returns X = tim, Y = cits3005, which is the supplementary list you wanted.

Checks

Read the two orderings aloud and the difference is obvious. The original asks "is there no pass anywhere, and also is there a sat?"; the fix asks "for each sat, is there no pass?" Negation binds nothing — the only way it succeeds is by its goal failing, and failure undoes bindings — so it can only ever be a test, never a generator. ✓

Why this matters

The rule: never put a negation before the goals that bind its variables. The bug has a name — floundering — and it is silent, which is why slide 42 asks the question rather than telling you the answer.

Slides 43–46. State the closed world assumption and the open world assumption, and prove that negation as failure commits you to the CWA. Then say why slide 46 calls this a problem in practice.

The two assumptions

CWA: the presumption that a statement that is true is also known to be true — and conversely, that what is not currently known to be true is false.

OWA: the only statements that are false are those that are provably false.

Proof that NAF commits you to the CWA

When we say P can be satisfied in Prolog, we mean there is some substitution for which P can be derived from the knowledge base. So \+P is true exactly when there is no substitution for which P can be derived.

That is a statement about the knowledge base, not the world. Treating it as $\neg P$ — which is what any program using \+ in a rule body does — is asserting that failure to derive implies falsity. That implication is the closed world assumption. ∎

Slide 43 states the gap directly: when we pose a query we are not asking "what is true in the world", we are asking "what can be truly derived from the knowledge base"; and if $\alpha$ cannot be derived, it does not follow that $\neg\alpha$ can be.

Why it is a problem

Because the CWA rarely applies to real knowledge bases. Slide 45's observation is that knowledge bases, formal and informal, record far more positive information than negative — so there is a natural asymmetry, and absence of a fact is usually ignorance rather than falsity. Negation in Prolog therefore breaks the OWA, which is the assumption real data actually satisfies.

Slide 44's quotation is the same point: the difficult category is the unknown unknowns, and a system that reports every unknown as false cannot distinguish them from the known-false.

Checks

Test it on G4's program. \+ pass(tim, cits3005) succeeds — but the knowledge base never said Tim failed; it simply has no pass fact for him. Under the CWA that is "Tim did not pass"; under the OWA it is "no idea". Same query, same output, entirely different epistemic status, and Prolog cannot tell you which you have. ✓

Lecture 3's nearby predicate is the same phenomenon — slide 43 says so explicitly. ✓

Why this matters

How to use this: for the homework, say in a comment which of your \+ uses rely on your facts being complete. A closed station list probably is complete; a list of who passed probably is not. That single sentence is the difference between using negation and understanding it.

G7recall

Slide 47. What is belief revision, what goes wrong without it, and what are the three operators?

What goes wrong

If a knowledge base contains an incorrect fact and it is inconsistent with other facts, all reasoning breaks down. From a contradiction everything follows, so an inconsistent knowledge base entails every sentence and its negation — the knowledge base still answers queries, and every answer is worthless.

Belief revision

The process of repairing a knowledge base when new information comes to light, or when correcting a misconception.

$K \oplus \varphi$extend a knowledge base by a fact
$K \ominus \varphi$retract a fact from a knowledge base
$K \otimes \varphi$revise a knowledge base with new information

The hard one is $\otimes$: it is non-trivial to decide which facts ought to be rescinded to allow the new information in. Several different retractions may restore consistency, and the logic does not say which is right.

Checks

Note how this closes the lecture's arc. The CWA says absent facts are false; belief revision asks what to do when present facts are wrong. Together they are the two ways a knowledge base can mislead you — by what it omits and by what it asserts — and neither is a problem the proof theory can solve, which is why they are representation questions rather than logic questions. ✓

Prolog's assert and retract are the $\oplus$ and $\ominus$ operators as programming constructs — and, per slide 35, are not part of definite clause logic. There is no built-in $\otimes$, because choosing what to give up is a design decision, not a computation. ✓
