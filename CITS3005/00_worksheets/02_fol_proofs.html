FOL proofs and proof trees

The four quantifier rules · building derivations · reading and drawing trees

Proofs only. This is the natural deduction half of Lecture 3, drilled — no semantics, no Prolog. It stands alongside the full Lecture 3 sheet rather than replacing it; sets are lettered so the numbering does not collide.

  1. Set A is reference — the rules as trees, and a search strategy that tells you which move to make from the shape of the goal. Read it once, then use it.
  2. B to F are derivations to build, roughly in increasing difficulty. Work them on paper before opening anything.
  3. G hands you broken proofs to diagnose. H is about which artefact to produce — tree or derivation — and converting between them.

Answers give the line-by-line derivation always, and the tree wherever branching makes the tree worth drawing. Where a claim is not provable, the answer gives a counter-interpretation instead — refuting is a skill in its own right and it gets tested.

A1 Before anything else: write the four rules out from memory, as trees, with their side conditions. Then check.

▶Check

Compare against the box above. Two things worth noticing while you check:

  • Only (∃E) has a subproof, and therefore only (∃E) carries a discharge label. The other three are one premise, one bar, one conclusion.
  • Freshness and clash are different conditions. Freshness is about the proof context — what else is currently assumed. Clash is about the formula — what is already bound inside it. A term can be perfectly fresh and still clash.

A2 The move you make is fixed by the shape of the goal, or of a premise. Complete the strategy table, then check it — this is the thing that turns proof-writing from guessing into procedure.

Work backwards from the goal, forwards from the premises, and meet. Backwards is the more reliable half: the goal's outermost symbol names the introduction rule that must produce it, and there is usually only one candidate.

The asymmetry to internalise: ∀ as a premise is generous — instantiate it however and however often you like. ∀ as a goal is demanding — you must prove it of something you know nothing about. ∃ is the mirror image: demanding as a goal (produce a witness), generous as a premise but only locally (you get a name, on loan).

α ∨ β tells you one disjunct holds but not which, so you show γ follows either way. ∃X.α(X) tells you some entity satisfies α but not which one — so you give it a temporary name Y, show β follows, and the result cannot have depended on which entity it was.

The difference is only that a disjunction has two cases to cover while an existential has one schematic case covering every entity at once. Hence one subproof rather than two.

And the freshness condition is the exact analogue of (∨E)'s requirement that both branches reach the same γ: in each case the conclusion must not record which choice was made.

B · One rule at a time

Short derivations. Each isolates a single quantifier rule so the side condition is easy to see.

B1 Derive ∀X.p(X) ⊢ p(a) and p(a) ⊢ ∃X.p(X). Draw both as trees.

▶Check
∀X.p(X)
∀E
p(a)
p(a)
∃I
∃X.p(X)

One step each. These are the two generous rules — no assumptions, no discharge, nothing to be careful about beyond clash, and a constant can never clash.

Read them together and the duality is plain: (∀E) turns a general claim into a particular one; (∃I) turns a particular claim into a general one. Same arrow, opposite directions.

B2 Derive ∀X.p(X) ⊢ ∃X.p(X). What assumption about the domain is this leaning on?

▶Check
1.∀X.p(X)hyp
2.p(Y)(∀E), 1
3.∃X.p(X)(∃I), 2

It leans on the domain being non-empty. Line 2 produces a term standing for an entity, and line 3 claims that entity exists. Over an empty domain ∀X.p(X) holds vacuously while ∃X.p(X) fails, so the sequent would be invalid.

Standard FOL stipulates a non-empty domain, so this is fine — but it is worth knowing that a stipulation rather than a rule is doing the work here. It is the one place the proof system quietly assumes something about the world.

B3 Derive ∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X)) ⊢ ∀X.p(X), and check the (∀I) condition explicitly.

▶Check
1.∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X))hyp
2.p(Y) ∧ q(Y)(∀E), 1
3.p(Y)(∧El), 2
4.∀X.p(X)(∀I), 3

The condition check: at line 4, the only undischarged hypothesis is line 1, which is a sentence — no free variables at all, so Y certainly is not free in it. Legal.

This is the shape most FOL proofs have: strip the quantifiers, do propositional work, put the quantifier back. Lines 2 and 4 are the wrapper; line 3 is the actual content.

The first (∃E) worth drawing. Note the subproof has been drawn out in full rather than as dots, because it is only two steps.

Y appears on lines 2, 3 and 4, and is gone by line 5. That is the whole discipline: the temporary name lives inside the subproof and nowhere else.

B5 Derive ∀X.(p(X) → q(X)), p(a) ⊢ q(a). Which term do you instantiate at, and how did you know?

▶Check
1.∀X.(p(X) → q(X))hyp

3.p(a) → q(a)(∀E), 1
4.q(a)(→E), 3, 2

Instantiate at a, because a is what the other premise is about and a is what the goal is about. That is the general heuristic: (∀E) offers you infinitely many choices, and the useful one is almost always a term already appearing in the goal or in another premise.

Section 8.6 of the Lecture 3 sheet is this observation from the other side — a machine cannot use the heuristic, which is why resolution computes the instantiation by unification instead of choosing it.

B6 Derive ∃X.∃Y.p(X,Y) ⊢ ∃Y.∃X.p(X,Y). Two nested (∃E)s — get the discharge labels right.

▶Check

Inner discharges first. Line 6 closes assumption 2 against the existential on line 2; line 7 closes assumption 1 against the original hypothesis. Subproofs nest — they never overlap.

Both freshness checks pass because the conclusion ∃Y.∃X.p(X,Y) is a sentence: neither W nor Z occurs in it. When your target is closed, freshness on (∃E) is automatic — which is worth knowing, because it means the condition only ever bites when you are trying to conclude something with a loose variable in it, and that is usually a sign you have stopped one step early.

C · Distributing quantifiers

Which quantifier distributes over which connective — and which does not. Two of these six are one-way only.

C1 Derive ∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X)) ⊢ ∀X.p(X) ∧ ∀X.q(X).

▶Check
1.∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X))hyp
2.p(Y) ∧ q(Y)(∀E), 1
3.p(Y)(∧El), 2
4.∀X.p(X)(∀I), 3
5.q(Y)(∧Er), 2
6.∀X.q(X)(∀I), 5
7.∀X.p(X) ∧ ∀X.q(X)(∧I), 4, 6

You may apply (∀I) to the same Y twice — nothing is consumed. Line 2 is also reused by lines 3 and 5. A derived line is a fact, available as often as you like, which is one respect in which a derivation is easier to write than the tree (where line 2 would have to be drawn twice).

C2 Now the converse: ∀X.p(X) ∧ ∀X.q(X) ⊢ ∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X)). Together with C1 this makes the two sides interderivable.

▶Check
1.∀X.p(X) ∧ ∀X.q(X)hyp
2.∀X.p(X)(∧El), 1
3.∀X.q(X)(∧Er), 1
4.p(Y)(∀E), 2
5.q(Y)(∀E), 3
6.p(Y) ∧ q(Y)(∧I), 4, 5
7.∀X.(p(X) ∧ q(X))(∀I), 6

Instantiate both at the same Y. That is the move — nothing forces it, but choosing different variables would leave you with p(Y) ∧ q(Z) and no way to proceed.

∀ distributes over ∧ in both directions, which fits the reading of ∀ as a big conjunction: reassociating a conjunction of conjunctions loses nothing.

Note the ⋮ columns: the two (∨E) branches are drawn as subproofs rather than in full. That is standard, and it is what keeps a tree readable — draw the branching structure, not every step.

Note also that the same Y runs through both branches. It was introduced once by (∃E); the case split afterwards is about which disjunct holds of it, not about which entity it is.

▶Check

Because the case split comes first. The premise is a disjunction of two separate existentials, so each branch has its own existential to unpack, and each needs its own witness name — Y in one branch, Z in the other. Contrast C3, where one (∃E) produced a single Y that both branches shared.

C3 and C4 together make the two sides interderivable, so ∃ distributes over ∨ both ways. Again the infinite-disjunction reading predicts it.

C5 Derive ∀X.p(X) ∨ ∀X.q(X) ⊢ ∀X.(p(X) ∨ q(X)). Then show the converse fails.

▶Check
1.∀X.p(X) ∨ ∀X.q(X)hyp
2.[∀X.p(X)]1assm
3.p(Y)(∀E), 2
4.p(Y) ∨ q(Y)(∨Il), 3
5.∀X.(p(X) ∨ q(X))(∀I), 4
6.[∀X.q(X)]2assm
7.q(Y)(∀E), 6
8.p(Y) ∨ q(Y)(∨Ir), 7
9.∀X.(p(X) ∨ q(X))(∀I), 8
10.∀X.(p(X) ∨ q(X))(∨E)1,2, 1, 2–5, 6–9

Check line 5 carefully, because it looks illegal and is not. (∀I) needs Y not free in any undischarged hypothesis; the open assumption at that point is [∀X.p(X)]1, which is a sentence. Y is not free in it. Legal.

The converse fails. Take DI = {1, 2} with pI true only at 1 and qI true only at 2. Every element satisfies p ∨ q, so the premise holds; but neither ∀X.p(X) nor ∀X.q(X) holds, so the conclusion fails.

So ∀ does not distribute over ∨. The reason is worth saying in words: the premise lets each element choose separately which disjunct it satisfies; the conclusion demands one choice made uniformly for all of them. Same structure as C6 and as the mixed-quantifier failures in set E — it is always about whether a choice may vary.

C6 Derive ∀X.(p(X) → q(X)) ⊢ ∀X.p(X) → ∀X.q(X), and show the converse fails.

▶Check
1.∀X.(p(X) → q(X))hyp
2.[∀X.p(X)]1assm
3.p(Y)(∀E), 2
4.p(Y) → q(Y)(∀E), 1
5.q(Y)(→E), 4, 3
6.∀X.q(X)(∀I), 5
7.∀X.p(X) → ∀X.q(X)(→I)1, 2–6

The converse fails. Same interpretation as C5: DI = {1, 2}, p true only at 1, q true only at 2. Then ∀X.p(X) is false, so ∀X.p(X) → ∀X.q(X) holds vacuously — but ∀X.(p(X) → q(X)) fails at element 1, where p holds and q does not.

The general lesson of set C. Quantifiers distribute freely over the connective they resemble — ∀ over ∧, ∃ over ∨ — and only one way over the other one. When a distribution fails, the counter-interpretation is almost always the same two-element domain with the two predicates true at opposite points. Keep it in your pocket; it refutes most false quantifier claims in one line.

D · Quantifier negation

The De Morgan laws for quantifiers. Three are constructive; one is not, and knowing which is the point.

D1 Derive ∀X.¬p(X) ⊢ ¬∃X.p(X).

▶Check
1.∀X.¬p(X)hyp
2.[∃X.p(X)]1assm
3.[p(Y)]2assm
4.¬p(Y)(∀E), 1
5.⊥(¬E), 3, 4
6.⊥(∃E)2, 2, 3–5
7.¬∃X.p(X)(¬I)1, 2–6

Goal is a negation, so (¬I) fires and you assume the negand — that is line 2, and it is forced, not chosen. Then the assumption is an existential, so (∃E) is likewise forced. The whole proof is determined by shapes.

Line 6 is the nicest freshness check you will ever do: β = ⊥ has no variables in it at all, so Y cannot possibly be free in it. Any (∃E) landing on ⊥ satisfies the condition automatically, which is why reductio proofs inside existentials go through so smoothly.

D2 Derive the converse, ¬∃X.p(X) ⊢ ∀X.¬p(X).

▶Check
1.¬∃X.p(X)hyp
2.[p(Y)]1assm
3.∃X.p(X)(∃I), 2
4.⊥(¬E), 3, 1
5.¬p(Y)(¬I)1, 2–4
6.∀X.¬p(X)(∀I), 5

The order at the end is the whole proof. Line 5 discharges assumption 1; only then is line 6 legal, since (∀I) needs Y free in nothing undischarged. Swap lines 5 and 6 and the proof is broken.

Compare D1, where (∃E) had to close before (¬I). In both cases the inner subproof closes first — subproofs nest, and a rule can only fire once everything it depends on is paid off.

Reading the tree top-down: the contradiction is manufactured at the (¬E) node from two leaves that are both assumptions, then (∃E) launders it into an unconditional ⊥, then (¬I) converts that into the negation. Two loans, two repayments, in the right order.

D4 Derive ¬∀X.p(X) ⊢ ∃X.¬p(X). This is the hard one — say what makes it different from D1–D3.

▶Check
1.¬∀X.p(X)hyp
2.[¬∃X.¬p(X)]1assm
3.[¬p(Y)]2assm
4.∃X.¬p(X)(∃I), 3
5.⊥(¬E), 4, 2

10.∃X.¬p(X)(PBC)1, 2–9

What is different: line 7. The other three directions are constructive — they build the thing they claim exists. This one cannot. From "not everything is p" you have no way to produce a counterexample; you can only show that the absence of one is contradictory. That takes double-negation elimination, and a second reductio wrapped around the first.

Line 8 is worth checking: (∀I) requires Y not free in any undischarged hypothesis. At that point the open ones are hypothesis 1 and assumption [¬∃X.¬p(X)]1 — both sentences. Assumption 2 was discharged at line 6. Legal.

Why this matters beyond the exercise. The constructive/classical split is exactly the difference between a proof that hands you a witness and one that merely rules out its absence. Lean's core logic is constructive and needs an explicit classical axiom for this direction — and Prolog, which only ever answers by producing a substitution, has no way to express this style of reasoning at all. That is one concrete face of the "decreases the expressive power" bullet on slide 42.

E · Mixed quantifiers and dependency

Swapping adjacent quantifiers. Like quantifiers commute freely; unlike ones do not, and the freshness conditions are what enforce it.

E1 Derive ∀X.∀Y.p(X,Y) ⊢ ∀Y.∀X.p(X,Y).

▶Check
1.∀X.∀Y.p(X,Y)hyp
2.∀Y.p(Z,Y)(∀E), 1
3.p(Z,W)(∀E), 2
4.∀X.p(X,W)(∀I), 3
5.∀Y.∀X.p(X,Y)(∀I), 4

Strip both, rebuild in the other order. Both (∀I) steps are legal because the only hypothesis is a sentence, so neither Z nor W is free in anything.

Adjacent like quantifiers always commute, in both directions, and the same proof works for ∃∃ (B6). No dependency exists between two universals, so there is nothing for an order to encode.

E2 Derive ∃X.∀Y.p(X,Y) ⊢ ∀Y.∃X.p(X,Y) — the valid mixed direction. Draw it as a tree and check both side conditions.

▶Check

2.[∀Y.p(Z,Y)]1assm
3.p(Z,W)(∀E), 2
4.∃X.p(X,W)(∃I), 3
5.∃X.p(X,W)(∃E)1, 1, 2–4

The order of the last two steps is the entire proof. (∃E) closes at line 5, and only then does (∀I) fire at line 6. In the tree, that is the (∃E) node sitting below the (∀I) bar — the existential is discharged first.

Reading it in words: you are handed one entity Z that works for every Y. Pick any Y at all, and Z still works for it. The witness never had to vary, which is why the proof goes through.

E3 Now attempt the converse, ∀Y.∃X.p(X,Y) ⊢ ∃X.∀Y.p(X,Y). Get as far as you can, then say precisely which condition stops you — and give a counter-interpretation.

▶Check
1.∀Y.∃X.p(X,Y)hyp
2.∃X.p(X,W)(∀E), 1
3.[p(Z,W)]1assm
4.∀Y.p(Z,Y)(∀I), 3   ← illegal
5.∃X.∀Y.p(X,Y)(∃I), 4
6.∃X.∀Y.p(X,Y)(∃E)1, 2, 3–5

Line 4 breaks. (∀I) generalises W, but W is free in the undischarged assumption [p(Z,W)]1. Freshness violated.

And the other route is blocked too. Close the (∃E) first and its conclusion would have to be p(Z,W) — but Z is free in that, violating the other freshness condition. The two conditions block the two possible orders, one each.

Counter-interpretation: DI = ℤ with pI as <. For every Y there is some X below it, so the premise holds; but no single integer is below all of them, so the conclusion fails.

This is the single most important thing in the set. In ∀Y.∃X the witness X may depend on Y; in ∃X.∀Y it may not. The freshness condition on (∀I) is precisely the mechanism that stops you laundering a dependent witness into an independent one — the proof system refuses for the same reason the semantics does. Skolemisation (slide 39) is the same fact a third time: the ∃ under a ∀ becomes a function of it, not a constant.

E4 Derive ∀X.p(X,X) ⊢ ∀X.∃Y.p(X,Y).

▶Check
1.∀X.p(X,X)hyp
2.p(Z,Z)(∀E), 1
3.∃Y.p(Z,Y)(∃I), 2
4.∀X.∃Y.p(X,Y)(∀I), 3

Short, but line 3 is the interesting step: the witness supplied to (∃I) is Z itself. Nothing requires the witness term to be different from anything else in the formula — it just has to be a term you have.

Note also that ∃X.∀Y.p(X,Y) does not follow here, by E3. "Everyone is related to themselves" does not give you one entity everyone is related to.

E5 Derive ∀X.(p(X) → ∀Y.q(Y)) ⊢ ∃X.p(X) → ∀Y.q(Y).

▶Check
1.∀X.(p(X) → ∀Y.q(Y))hyp
2.[∃X.p(X)]1assm
3.[p(Z)]2assm
4.p(Z) → ∀Y.q(Y)(∀E), 1
5.∀Y.q(Y)(→E), 4, 3
6.∀Y.q(Y)(∃E)2, 2, 3–5
7.∃X.p(X) → ∀Y.q(Y)(→I)1, 2–6

The (∃E) at line 6 lands on a quantified β, which is fine — the condition is only that Z is not free in it, and ∀Y.q(Y) is a sentence.

Worth noticing that no (∀I) appears anywhere. The ∀Y.q(Y) in the conclusion was never built; it came out of the premise whole and was carried through. Not every quantifier in a goal has to be introduced — check whether one is already available before you start proving it from scratch.

F · Arguments

Translate, then derive. Formalising is half the work, and a bad translation makes a valid argument unprovable.

F1 Everyone who studies CITS3005 studies logic. Maria studies CITS3005. Therefore someone studies logic.

▶Check

Writing s(X,U) for "X studies U":

∀X.(s(X, cits3005) → s(X, logic)),   s(maria, cits3005)   ⊢   ∃X.s(X, logic)
1.∀X.(s(X,cits3005) → s(X,logic))hyp
2.s(maria, cits3005)hyp
3.s(maria,cits3005) → s(maria,logic)(∀E), 1
4.s(maria, logic)(→E), 3, 2
5.∃X.s(X, logic)(∃I), 4

Instantiate at maria because that is the constant the second premise supplies, and use her again as the (∃I) witness. No assumptions, no discharge, no side conditions — arguments about named individuals stay in the easy fragment.

Check the translation of the first premise: ∀ with →. Using ∧ would assert that everything in the domain studies CITS3005, including Maria's bicycle.

F3 Everyone loves someone. Therefore someone is loved by everyone. Prove it or refute it.

▶Check
∀X.∃Y.loves(X,Y)   ⊢   ∃Y.∀X.loves(X,Y)

Invalid. Take a domain of two people who each love only themselves: DI = {a, b} with lovesI true exactly at (a,a) and (b,b). Everyone loves someone, but nobody is loved by everyone.

Where an attempted proof dies: after (∀E) and opening [loves(Z,W)]1 by (∃E), you would want (∀I) on Z to get ∀X.loves(X,W) — but Z is free in that open assumption. Freshness violated. It is E3 wearing different clothes.

Sanity check every quantifier swap before you try to prove it. The two-element counter-model above refutes most of them in one line, and it is far cheaper than half an hour spent trying to make an illegal (∀I) look legal. If a proof keeps failing at the same side condition, stop proving and start refuting.

F4 No student is a lecturer. Some student teaches. Therefore someone who teaches is not a lecturer.

▶Check
∀X.(st(X) → ¬lec(X)),   ∃X.(st(X) ∧ t(X))   ⊢   ∃X.(t(X) ∧ ¬lec(X))
1.∀X.(st(X) → ¬lec(X))hyp
2.∃X.(st(X) ∧ t(X))hyp
3.[st(Y) ∧ t(Y)]1assm
4.st(Y)(∧El), 3

7.¬lec(Y)(→E), 6, 4
8.t(Y) ∧ ¬lec(Y)(∧I), 5, 7
9.∃X.(t(X) ∧ ¬lec(X))(∃I), 8
10.∃X.(t(X) ∧ ¬lec(X))(∃E)1, 2, 3–9

The full (∃E) pattern, and the one to be able to write without thinking: open the existential, do all the work on the named witness, repackage with (∃I), discharge. Lines 3 and 9–10 are the wrapper; 4–8 are the argument.

Line 10 is legal because the conclusion is a sentence. If you stopped at line 8 and tried to discharge, Y would be free in β — which is exactly the error in G2, and it is the error people actually make: forgetting to repackage before closing.

Note "no student is a lecturer" becomes ∀X.(st(X) → ¬lec(X)), not ¬∀X.(st(X) → lec(X)). "No A is B" is a universal negative, and getting it wrong makes the argument unprovable rather than merely awkward.

G · Diagnose the broken step

Each of these is illegal. Name the violated condition, and say what the proof would otherwise have established.

G1 What is wrong here, and why is the conclusion false?

[p(Y)]1
∀I
∀X.p(X)
→I1
p(Y) → ∀X.p(X)

▶Check

The (∀I) step. Y is free in the assumption [p(Y)]1, which is still open at that point — it is not discharged until the (→I) below. Freshness violated.

The conclusion says: if any one thing is p, everything is. False in any interpretation where p holds of some but not all — the two-element model again.

The reason the tree makes this easy to see: an assumption stays visibly bracketed at the leaf until the bar that discharges it. Anything between the leaf and that bar is inside its scope. Here the (∀I) bar sits inside the scope of [p(Y)]1, which is exactly what freshness forbids.

G2 And here?

∃X.p(X)
[p(Y)]1
⋮
p(Y)
∃E1
p(Y)

▶Check

The (∃E) step. Y is free in β, which is p(Y) itself. Violated.

It would establish ∃X.p(X) ⊢ p(Y) — "something is p, therefore this thing is." The temporary name has escaped the subproof it was defined in.

The repair is always the same: one more (∃I) before discharging, so β becomes ∃X.p(X) and Y disappears. See F4, where that repackaging step is line 9. A subproof that ends holding the witness has stopped one step too early.

G3 From ∀Y.loves(Y,Y) — everyone loves themselves — someone infers ∃X.∀Y.loves(Y,X) by (∃I) "with t = Y". Diagnose it.

▶Check

Clash. (∃I) goes from α(t) to ∃X.α(X), where α(t) is what you get by putting t into α(X). Here α(X) = ∀Y.loves(Y,X), so substituting t = Y would put Y inside the scope of ∀Y — it goes in free and comes out bound. ∀Y.loves(Y,Y) is therefore not a legal instance of α(t) at all, and the side condition rules it out.

And it is genuinely invalid: two people who each love only themselves satisfy the premise, and nobody is loved by everyone.

The fix, when a clash is the only problem, is free: rename the bound variable first. Here renaming does not rescue the inference, because the inference is wrong on the merits — which is the general pattern. The side conditions do not block harmless moves; if one fires, look for the counter-interpretation.

G4 From the hypothesis p(a), someone applies (∀I) to conclude ∀X.p(X). Why is that not licensed?

▶Check

Because a is not arbitrary — it occurs in the hypothesis. The freshness condition says the generalised symbol must not appear free in any hypothesis, and here the hypothesis is about a. Everything you know about a came from a premise specific to it.

The rule as stated also generalises a variable Y, not a constant. That is not an accident of notation: the variable is a placeholder standing for an arbitrary entity, which is precisely the status a named constant does not have.

Invalid, obviously — one thing being p says nothing about the rest. Contrast B3, where (∀I) is applied to a Y that arrived only by (∀E) from a universal hypothesis, so nothing special was ever assumed about it.

One question catches all four of these: where did this variable come from, and is anything currently assumed about it? G1 and G4 generalise something that came from an assumption; G2 lets something from an assumption escape; G3 lets something walk into a binder. Ask it at every quantifier step and you will not lose these marks.

H · Trees and derivations

Two presentations of the same object. Being able to move between them, and knowing which to hand in.

H1 When is a tree the better artefact, and when is a line-by-line derivation? What decides which one you produce?

▶Check

What decides it is the question. If it says "proof tree" in those words, draw one; otherwise a derivation with rule citations is a complete natural deduction proof and nothing is missing. The lecture presents both formats and treats them as interchangeable.

Trees earn their space when branching is the point. (∨E) and (∃E) with subproofs converging on one conclusion — C3 is the clearest case in this sheet — because the picture shows which assumption belongs to which branch, where a derivation records it only in the line ranges.

Derivations win when the proof is a straight line, and when anything gets reused. A tree has no way to refer back to an earlier result: it must redraw the whole subtree, and hypotheses reappear at every leaf that needs them (H3). A chain like F1 or E1 blows up horizontally in tree form for no gain.

Practical default: derive linearly, and reach for the tree when you count two or more open assumptions at once.

Method: read the tree bottom-up to find the last rule applied, then write the derivation top-down, doing each subtree before the node beneath it. Leaves become hypotheses or bracketed assumptions; each bar becomes one numbered line citing the lines its premises landed on.

The one thing the tree does not tell you directly is the line range 3–6. You have to read it off the shape: the subproof runs from the bracketed leaf down to the node just above the discharging bar. That range is the only record in a derivation that lines 3 to 6 lived under an assumption — which is why omitting it is an error, not sloppiness.
