A2prove

Slide 4 gives this Prolog knowledge base:

red(apple). green(apple). red(tomato). fruit(apple).
redFruit(X) :- fruit(X), red(X).

Show that the last line cannot be expressed in propositional logic. Argue from the definition of a propositional atom, and give two distinct reasons.

Set-up

A propositional atom is an unanalysed Boolean: it is true or false and has no internal parts. red(apple) looks atomic but is not — it asserts a relation between a named thing and a property, and the rule needs access to that structure.

Proof

First reason — no shared structure. Propositionally, red(apple) and red(tomato) are two unrelated letters $p$ and $q$. Nothing connects them, so no propositional formula can range over "whatever is red". The rule's content is precisely that redness is one property holding of several things, and that fact is invisible to a logic whose atoms have no parts.

Second reason — no unbounded domain. You can fake the rule for a fixed finite list of entities by writing one implication each:

$$ (fruit_{apple} \wedge red_{apple} \to redFruit_{apple}) \wedge (fruit_{tomato} \wedge red_{tomato} \to redFruit_{tomato}) \wedge \cdots $$

This step is exactly where the finiteness of the domain is used: the conjunction must be written out, so the entities must be known in advance and there must be finitely many. Slide 5 puts the contrast directly — propositional logic holds a finite number of Boolean concepts, while first order logic considers unbounded sets of entities.

Why this matters

The moral: FOL buys exactly one thing here, and it is the quantifier — a proposition that depends on the entire domain rather than on isolated facts. The second reason is the load-bearing one: the first is an inconvenience, the second is an impossibility.

A4prove

Slide 7 gives the family tree language $C = \{john, jane, cris \dots\}$, $F = \{mother, father\}$, $P = \{ancestor, male, related\}$.

  1. Give the arity of every symbol in $F$ and $P$, and write one well-formed sentence of this language.
  2. $mother$ is a function here. Show that this commits the model to a claim about the domain that a binary predicate $mother(X,Y)$ would not, and say which claim.
  3. Give one situation each modelling choice handles better than the other.

For (b), look at the type a function symbol is given on slide 11 and ask what it means for that map to be defined on every tuple.

(a)

$\#mother = \#father = 1$; $\#male = 1$; $\#ancestor = \#related = 2$. A sentence: $\forall X. ancestor(mother(X), X)$ — everyone's mother is an ancestor of them.

(b)

Slide 11 requires $mother^{I} : D^{I} \longrightarrow D^{I}$, and this is a function in the mathematical sense: total and single-valued.

Total means defined on every element of the domain. So the model asserts that every entity has a mother — including $jane$'s mother, and her mother, and so on without end. If the domain is finite the chain must therefore cycle, which is absurd for a family tree; if it is infinite you have committed to infinitely many people. This step is exactly where the totality of $f^{I}$ is used, and it is not optional: if $mother^{I}$ were undefined anywhere, the term $mother(X)$ would name nothing and slide 13's environment clause would have nothing to return.

Single-valued means exactly one mother each — which is fine biologically and wrong for adoptive or legal parenthood.

A binary predicate $mother(X,Y)$ asserts neither. Some entities may have no recorded mother, and some may have two.

(c)

Function is better when you want to build terms: $ancestor(mother(mother(X)), X)$ is compact, and the single-valuedness is doing useful work. The predicate version forces you to introduce and quantify a variable for every step.

Predicate is better for a real database, where the natural state is incomplete — most records have no mother listed, and a total function has no way to say "unknown" without inventing a domain element to mean it.

Why this matters

The moral: neither choice is wrong, and the logic will not tell you which you wanted. The choice is a claim about the world, and you make it the moment you put a symbol in $F$ rather than $P$. G6 is the same decision again, about grades.

A5recall

Slide 5 says first order logic is "just expressive enough to describe the state of the world, what is". Name two things it therefore cannot express, and say what feature of the syntax rules each of them out.

Answer

Second order claims. "Every property that holds of Tim also holds of Jane" needs $\forall P$, quantifying over predicate symbols. Slide 8's formula BNF admits only $\forall X.\alpha$ with $X \in var$, and $var$ contains variables, not predicate symbols. Ruled out by the quantifier production. B5 makes you check exactly this.

Modality. What must be, what could be, what an agent believes or intends, what will be true tomorrow. There is no production in the BNF for any such operator — the grammar offers $\neg, \wedge, \vee, \to$ and the two quantifiers, and none of them can be given that reading. FOL describes one static state of affairs.

Why this matters

How to use this: the restriction is deliberate rather than an oversight. Slide 18's undecidability result is already the price of the expressiveness FOL does have, so adding more makes automated reasoning worse, not better. Part G is the same trade in the other direction — Prolog gives up expressiveness to buy a workable proof procedure.

Part B · Terms, formulae and what they can say

Slides 8–9. The one idea to hold onto: terms name things, formulae are true or false, and the two grammars meet at exactly one production. B1 is the discrimination drill and B6 is the one worth most — slide 9's own exercise, where the honest answer is that the sentence you are asked to write is false.

B3recall

Slide 8 notes that a formula with a free variable is still legal under the BNF. State the definition of a sentence, and say why the semantics needs the distinction at all.

Answer

A variable occurrence in $\alpha$ not inside the scope of a quantifier binding it is free; a formula with no free variables is a sentence.

The semantics needs it because an interpretation alone gives no meaning to a free variable. Slide 11 has $I$ assigning an entity to each constant, a function to each function symbol and a truth function to each predicate symbol — and saying nothing whatever about $X$. So $p(X)$ has no truth value under $I$.

That gap is what the environment $\theta$ fills, and it is why satisfaction is written $I \overset{\theta}{\models} \alpha$ in general. Slide 14 permits dropping the $\theta$ in exactly one case: when $\alpha$ has no free variables.

Why this matters

The moral, in one line: sentences are true or false; open formulae are only true of things.

B4compute

Show that $\forall X. \exists Y.\bigl(p(X, f(Y)) \to q(c)\bigr)$ is generated by slide 8's BNF. Work outside in, naming the production used at each step, and say whether it is a sentence.

Proof
  1. $\forall X.\alpha$ with $\alpha = \exists Y.(\dots)$ — the formula production for $\forall$.
  2. $\exists Y.\beta$ with $\beta = p(X, f(Y)) \to q(c)$ — the formula production for $\exists$.
  3. $\beta$ is $\alpha_1 \to \alpha_2$ — the implication production.
  4. $\alpha_1 = p(\tau_1, \tau_2)$ — the bridge production, with $\tau_1 = X$ by the variable rule and $\tau_2 = f(Y)$ by the function rule applied to the variable rule.
  5. $\alpha_2 = q(c)$ — the bridge production again, with $\tau = c$ by the constant rule.
Checks

Both branches bottom out in the term BNF, which is what "well formed" means. Every quantifier binds a variable and every variable occurrence lies inside a matching scope, so it is a sentence. ✓

Second check: count the bridge productions. There are two, one per predicate application, and each appears exactly where the derivation crosses from truth values back down to entities. A well-formed formula has at least one; a term has none. ✓

B5prove

Slide 9's exercise, part one and part two. Express each in first order logic over the arithmetic language of slide 7, and justify the choice of connective in each.

  1. Every even number is followed by an odd number.
  2. For every number there is always a prime larger than it.
(a) $$ \forall X.\bigl(even(X) \to odd(succ(X))\bigr) $$

Two decisions. "Followed by" becomes the function $succ$ rather than a predicate, because you want a term denoting the next number so that $odd$ can be applied to it — a predicate $follows(Y,X)$ would work but forces an extra quantifier and an extra variable.

And $\forall$ pairs with $\to$. This step is exactly where the guard is needed: you are constraining the even numbers, not asserting that everything is even. $\forall X.(even(X) \wedge odd(succ(X)))$ says every entity in the domain is even, which is false the moment the domain contains anything odd.

(b) $$ \forall X. \exists Y.\bigl(prime(Y) \wedge X \lt Y\bigr) $$

Here $\exists$ pairs with $\wedge$. This step is exactly where the conjunction is needed: $\exists Y.(prime(Y) \to X \lt Y)$ is satisfied by any non-prime $Y$, since a false antecedent makes the implication true, so it claims almost nothing.

The quantifier order matters too, and it is the intended reading: $\forall X. \exists Y$ lets the prime depend on $X$, which is what makes the sentence true. See D2.

Checks

Test the pairing rule against a one-element domain $D^{I} = \{0\}$ with $even^{I}(0) = \top$, $odd^{I}(0) = \bot$. The intended (a) is false there, correctly, since $succ^{I}(0)$ must be $0$ and $0$ is not odd. The mis-paired $\forall X.(even(X) \wedge odd(succ(X)))$ is also false — so this domain does not separate them. A domain containing one even and one odd element does. ✓

Why this matters

The common error is the $\exists \dots \to$ form in (b). It is the single most frequent formalisation mistake, and the reason is that English "there is a prime such that" sounds like a conditional when it is a conjunction.

B6prove

Slide 9's exercise, part three: every number is the square of some different number.

  1. Express it in first order logic.
  2. Decide whether your sentence is true of the natural numbers, and prove your answer.
  3. Say what (b) tells you about what the exercise was asking for.

For (b) you only need one counterexample, and the smallest one is very small. For (c), reread the instruction: it says "express", not "prove".

(a) $$ \forall X. \exists Y.\bigl(\neg (X = Y) \wedge X = Y * Y\bigr) $$ (b)

It is false over $\mathbb{N}$. Take $X = 3$. The candidates $Y$ with $Y * Y \le 3$ are $0$ and $1$, giving squares $0$ and $1$; every larger $Y$ gives a square of at least $4$. So no $Y$ has $3 = Y*Y$, and the inner conjunct fails for every $Y$ regardless of the inequality. Hence $\exists Y$ fails at $X = 3$, so $\forall X$ fails. ∎

It is false over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ as well, for a different reason. There the square root exists, but at $X = 0$ the only $Y$ with $Y^2 = 0$ is $0$ itself, and the sentence demands a different one. So the failure is at $0$ rather than at $3$ — a good illustration that changing the domain changes which entity witnesses the failure, not just whether there is one.

(c)

The exercise asked you to express the property, not to assert something true. A sentence's truth is relative to an interpretation and the syntax has no opinion about it, so writing a false sentence is a perfectly good outcome here.

Why this matters

The common error is to notice the sentence comes out false and start editing the formula until it comes out true — dropping the $\neg(X=Y)$, or weakening $\forall$ to $\exists$. That is a category error: it replaces the property you were asked to express with a different property that happens to hold. The same instinct will later make you distrust correct Prolog rules that return false, which is G3.

C2compute

Language: $C = \{a, b\}$, $F = \{f\}$ with $\#f = 1$, $P = \{p\}$ with $\#p = 1$. Construct an interpretation with a two-element domain in which $\forall X. p(f(X))$ is true and $\forall X. p(X)$ is false. Give all four components and verify both claims.

Set-up

$D^{I} = \{1, 2\}$; $a^{I} = 1$, $b^{I} = 2$; $f^{I}(1) = f^{I}(2) = 1$; $p^{I}(1) = \top$, $p^{I}(2) = \bot$.

Verification

For $\forall X.p(f(X))$: by the $\forall$ clause this holds iff $I \overset{\theta[X \mapsto d]}{\models} p(f(X))$ for all $d \in D^{I}$. By the predicate clause that is $p^{I}(\theta(f(X))) = p^{I}(f^{I}(d))$. Now $f^{I}(1) = 1$ and $f^{I}(2) = 1$, and $p^{I}(1) = \top$ in both cases. Holds for every $d$, so the sentence is true. ✓

For $\forall X.p(X)$: take $d = 2$. Then $p^{I}(2) = \bot$, so the clause fails at $d = 2$ and the sentence is false. ✓

Checks

Independent check by counting. $f^{I}$ has image $\{1\}$, and $p^{I}$ is true exactly on $\{1\}$, so $p(f(X))$ is true at every $d$ whose image lands in the true set — which is all of them. Meanwhile $p^{I}$ is true on one of two elements, so a universal over $p$ alone must fail. The two answers are forced by the image of $f^{I}$ being a proper subset of the domain. ✓

Why this matters

How to use this: constructing a separating interpretation is the FOL version of producing the truth-table row that breaks an argument, and it is the standard way to show something is not valid. You will use it in D3, D4 and E7. The recipe that answers most such questions: a two-element domain, with the two predicates true at opposite points.

Decide each, with proof or counterexample.

  1. May two distinct constant symbols denote the same entity?
  2. May an entity have no constant symbol naming it?
  3. Is $\neg(a = b)$ a logical truth?
  4. Must $f^{I}$ be defined on every tuple of domain elements, and what breaks if it is not?
(a) and (b)

Yes to both. Slide 11 asks only for an element $c^{I} \in D^{I}$ for each $c \in C$ — that is, a function from $C$ to $D^{I}$. Nothing requires it to be injective, so $tim^{I} = tim\_french^{I}$ is permitted; nothing requires it to be surjective either, and slide 6 says so outright: you can reason about entities without every one being labelled.

(c)

No. By (a), take $D^{I} = \{1\}$ with $a^{I} = b^{I} = 1$ and $=^{I}$ the identity relation. Then $a = b$ is satisfied, so $\neg(a=b)$ is false in this interpretation, so it is not valid. ∎

If you want distinct names to denote distinct things you must assert it, as the unique names assumption. Prolog builds it in for atoms, which is one of the places its behaviour and plain FOL come apart — see G4.

(d)

Yes, it must be total. Suppose $f^{I}$ were undefined on some tuple $\vec{d}$. Answer $$ I \overset{\theta}{\models} \forall X.\alpha \iff I \overset{\theta[X \mapsto d]}{\models} \alpha \ \text{ for all } d \in D^{I} $$ $$ I \overset{\theta}{\models} \exists X.\alpha \iff \text{there exists some } d \in D^{I} \text{ such that } I \overset{\theta[X \mapsto d]}{\models} \alpha $$

How to use this: "otherwise identical" is what makes nested quantifiers work. In $\forall X.\exists Y.\alpha$ the inner clause produces $\theta[X \mapsto d][Y \mapsto e]$, and $X$ keeps the value the outer quantifier gave it. If the override were a reset, the inner formula could not refer to $X$ at all, and D2's unfolding would not terminate in anything meaningful.

D4prove

Each of the following is false. For each, name the confusion or the dropped hypothesis, and give an explicit counterexample.

  1. $\forall X.(p(X) \vee q(X))$ entails $\forall X.p(X) \vee \forall X.q(X)$.
  2. If $\exists X.p(X)$ and $\exists X.q(X)$ both hold, then so does $\exists X.(p(X) \wedge q(X))$.
  3. $\forall X. \exists Y.\, p(X,Y)$ entails $\exists Y. \forall X.\, p(X,Y)$.
  4. A formula true in some interpretation is valid.
  5. Since validity in first order logic is undecidable, there is no way to determine whether any particular formula is valid.

One interpretation kills (a) and (b) at once, and it is the one from C2. For (c), think about an order with no least element.

(a)

The confusion: the premise lets each element choose separately which disjunct it satisfies; the conclusion demands one choice made uniformly for all of them.

Counterexample: $D^{I} = \{1,2\}$, $p^{I}$ true only at $1$, $q^{I}$ true only at $2$. Every element satisfies $p \vee q$, so the premise holds. But $\forall X.p(X)$ fails at $2$ and $\forall X.q(X)$ fails at $1$, so the conclusion fails. ∎

(The converse does hold, which is why the claim sounds right.)

(b)

The confusion: two existentials produce two unrelated witnesses, and nothing identifies them.

Counterexample: the same interpretation. $1$ witnesses $\exists X.p(X)$ and $2$ witnesses $\exists X.q(X)$, but no single element satisfies both, so $\exists X.(p(X) \wedge q(X))$ fails. ∎

(c)

The confusion: in $\forall X.\exists Y$ the witness may depend on $X$; in $\exists Y.\forall X$ it may not.

Counterexample: $D^{I} = \mathbb{Z}$, $p^{I}$ the relation $\lt$. For every $Y$ there is some $X$ below it, so the premise holds. But no integer is below every integer, so the conclusion fails. ∎ (Over $\mathbb{N}$ this counterexample would not work, since $0$ has nothing below it — the premise would already be false. Choosing $\mathbb{Z}$ is doing real work.)

(d)

The confusion: satisfiable with valid. Satisfiable is true in some interpretation; valid is true in every one.

Counterexample: $\exists X.p(X) \to \forall X.p(X)$ is true wherever $p^{I}$ holds of everything or of nothing, so it is satisfiable. It is false in C2's interpretation, where $p^{I}$ holds at $1$ but not $2$. So satisfiable and not valid. ∎

(e)

The confusion: a property of the general problem with a property of each instance. Undecidability says no single procedure settles every formula; it does not say any particular formula resists settling.

Counterexample: $\forall X.(p(X) \vee \neg p(X))$ is valid, and you can see that it is in one line. Moreover slide 18 says validity is semi-decidable: an exhaustive natural deduction search will eventually produce every valid formula, so any given valid formula is confirmed in finite time. What you cannot do is recognise invalidity by waiting. ∎

Why this matters

The moral: (a), (b) and (c) are the same mistake wearing three coats — treating a choice that may vary as though it were fixed. Notice that one two-element interpretation refutes two of them, and keep it in your pocket: it settles most false quantifier claims in a line. D6recall

Slide 18 says validity in first order logic is undecidable but semi-decidable. State exactly what a machine can and cannot do, say why propositional logic is not like this, and state the relationship between validity and unsatisfiability that automated reasoning exploits.

Answer

Can: run an infinite process generating every valid formula of first order logic — applying natural deduction exhaustively is one such process. So if $\alpha$ is valid it will eventually appear, and you will know.

Cannot: confirm that $\alpha$ is not valid. That would require the generation process to finish without producing it, and the process never finishes. You are left unable to distinguish "not valid" from "not found yet".

That asymmetry is what semi-decidable names: the yes-instances are recognisable, the no-instances are not.

Why propositional logic escapes

A propositional formula contains finitely many atoms, so there are finitely many assignments; build the truth table, read the answer, always terminates. First order quantifiers range over domains of unbounded size, so there is no finite object to check. This is also why slide 14's satisfaction relation is defined recursively rather than by a table (C4): it defines truth without ever computing it.

The relationship $$ \alpha \text{ is valid} \iff \neg\alpha \text{ is unsatisfiable}, \qquad\text{and more usefully}\qquad S \models \alpha \iff S \cup \{\neg\alpha\} \text{ is unsatisfiable} $$

This is what resolution exploits. Instead of constructing a proof of $\alpha$, negate the goal, add it to the premises, and hunt for a contradiction — converting an open-ended search for a derivation into a search for one specific thing.

Why this matters

How to use this: "undecidable" is a statement about the general problem, not about your formula — D4(e). Useful fragments are decidable outright, which is exactly why the unit later restricts to definite clauses (G7) and, after that, to description logics. Restriction is how you buy back decidability.

Proof

Scope enforces it. y is bound by the inner fun, so it exists only within those parentheses. The result type of Exists.elim is fixed outside that scope as ∃ x, B x — a type in which y cannot occur, because y is not in scope where that type is written.

Slide 32 defines nearby with two clauses:

Say why this is two clauses rather than one clause with a disjunction in the body. Then state exactly what the second clause requires, and name two kinds of "two stops away" it does not capture.

Why two clauses

Because Prolog is definite clausal logic: every clause has exactly one positive literal and its body is a conjunction, so there is no $\vee$ available inside a body. Multiple clauses with the same head are how disjunction is expressed — slide 37 says so directly, describing rules as implications with only one atomic predicate as a conclusion, with potentially multiple rules for the same conclusion. Operationally this is what backtracking is: try clause one, and on failure retry with clause two.

What the second clause requires

An intermediate station Z and the same line L for both legs — the variable is shared, so unification forces the match.

What it misses

Line changes. Bond Street to Oxford Circus is central; Oxford Circus to Piccadilly Circus is bakerloo. Two stops, different lines, so nearby fails. Whether that is a bug depends on what you meant: if "nearby" means two stops without changing, the rule is right and the name misleads.

Direction. Every connected fact runs one way, so nearby(oxford_circus, bond_street) fails though nearby(bond_street, oxford_circus) succeeds. The rule cannot fix this — the facts encode a direction they should not.

Why this matters

The moral: nothing here is malfunctioning. The knowledge base answers exactly the question it encodes, and the gap is between that and the question you thought you were asking. Reading a rule back in English and testing it on a case you did not design for is the whole skill.

Clause 1 is vacuous since $person$ is false; clause 2 vacuous since $bored$ is false; clause 3 vacuous since $old$ is false; clause 4 holds; clause 5 holds since $sleeping$ is false; clause 6 holds. All six satisfied, so the program is satisfiable. ✓

(c)

$\neg old$ is entailed. Clause 6 gives $awake$, so clause 5 forces $\neg sleeping$. Suppose $old$. Clause 3 gives $bored$; clause 2 then gives $sleeping \vee young$, and since $sleeping$ is false, $young$. But then $young \wedge old$ contradicts clause 4. So $old$ is false in every model. ∎

$\neg bored$ is not entailed. $\{awake, bored, young\}$ is also a model: clause 1 vacuous, clause 2 satisfied since $young$ holds, clause 3 vacuous since $old$ is false, clause 4 holds since $old$ is false, clause 5 holds, clause 6 holds. Since $bored$ is true in this model and false in (b)'s, neither $bored$ nor $\neg bored$ is entailed. ∎

(d)

Slide 42 forbids :- married(X)., which has zero positive literals, and teaches(X,course(Z)); supervises(X,Z) :- student_of(X,Z), which has two. Full clausal logic permits such indefinite clauses, for example married(peter); bachelor(peter)., saying one holds without saying which.

The restriction to definite clauses: decreases the expressive power of the logic; does not affect the soundness, completeness or decidability results from clausal logic; and gives a clear systematic way to apply resolution, the rule Prolog uses to execute programs. The intuition behind the third: with exactly one positive literal per clause a goal always matches a unique head, so the engine never reasons by cases about which disjunct holds.

Checks

Cross-check (c) against G3. Here two models disagree about $bored$, so the honest answer is that it is undecided — and clausal logic says so. Prolog, asked the same thing, would print false, which conflates "false in every model" with "not derivable". The difference is the closed-world assumption, and this program is the cleanest place in the lecture to see it. ✓

Why this matters

The moral: a Prolog query is itself the one clause the restriction forbids — a goal is added as the negated goal, a clause with no positive literal, and by D6 deriving a contradiction from it is what proves the query. So the program is definite; the question is not.

H3prove

Derive $\exists X.p(X) \vee \exists X.q(X)$ from $\exists X.\bigl(p(X) \vee q(X)\bigr)$. Then explain why both branches use the same variable, and why the converse also holds while the corresponding claim for $\forall$ does not.

Derivation

Why the same variable

Because $(\exists E)$ names the witness once, at line 2, and hands you $\alpha(Y)$ where $\alpha$ is whatever sat under the quantifier — here the disjunction $p(X) \vee q(X)$, so you receive $p(Y) \vee q(Y)$.

Lines 3 and 6 are then the two ways that sentence can be true, so they are claims about $Y$. This step is exactly where the single witness is used: $(\vee E)$ requires its two case assumptions to be the disjuncts of the disjunction it is splitting, and that disjunction is about $Y$. Using two names would be splitting $p(Y) \vee q(Z)$, which you were never given — and which is the conclusion, not the premise.

There are two distinct ignorances here, handled by two distinct rules: which entity, handled by $(\exists E)$, and then which disjunct, handled by $(\vee E)$. The nesting order follows, since you cannot split a disjunction before you have one.

The converse, and the contrast

The converse holds too, so the two are interderivable — but it needs two $(\exists E)$ steps, because there the case split comes first and each branch carries its own existential to unpack, hence its own witness name.

The corresponding $\forall$ claim fails in one direction: $\forall X.(p(X) \vee q(X))$ does not entail $\forall X.p(X) \vee \forall X.q(X)$, refuted by the interpretation of H2(a) and H2(b). $\exists$ distributes over $\vee$ both ways; $\forall$ does not.

Why this matters

The moral: $\forall$ behaves like a big $\wedge$ and $\exists$ like a big $\vee$, and each distributes freely over the connective it resembles. When a distribution fails, it is because a choice that was allowed to vary with the element is being required to be uniform.
